# Covenant Identity — Desired Outcomes of a Biblically Aligned Identity
## What Becomes Visible When the Interior Life Meets an Actual Life

---

## Desired, Not Promised

This document names outcomes — not promises, not a checklist, not a bar to be measured against. That distinction has to be established before anything else here can be read safely.

[[Covenant Identity — The Interior Life as Telos]] portrays what happens on the inside of a person when identity is received rather than performed. This document asks the next question: what does that produce when it meets an actual life — a marriage, a job, a checking account, a hard conversation, a season of loss, a room full of people who can't do anything for you? The two documents describe one reality from two angles. Nothing here is a separate achievement layered on top of the interior work; it is what the interior work looks like from outside the person having it.

But "what it looks like" is not the same as "what is guaranteed," and the difference matters enough to state plainly.

Paul names the fruit of the Spirit in the same breath as this warning: *"For the desires of the flesh are against the Spirit, and the desires of the Spirit are against the flesh, for these are opposed to each other, to keep you from doing the things you want to do"* (Gal 5:17 ESV). The fruit is real, and so is the ongoing opposition. A person can be genuinely being transformed and still be in a real fight — the fruit is evidence the Spirit is at work, not proof the war is over. Anyone reading this document as a scorecard for whether they (or a client) have "arrived" has already misread it.

Jesus told a parable about this exact unevenness. The same good seed, sown in the same good soil, produced *"thirtyfold and sixty and a hundred"* (Mk 4:8 ESV) — not because some of the soil was more faithful than the rest, but because yield varies even where the soil is genuinely good. A person can show real, recognizable fruit in patience and still be visibly stuck in how they handle money. That is not evidence the identity work isn't real. It is what growth in a fallen world actually looks like while it is still happening.

Jonathan Edwards spent the better part of *Religious Affections* (1746) working out exactly this tension, and his answer is the theological backbone of this document. Edwards names Christian practice — a life that is actually, visibly different, sustained over time — as the final and decisive sign that separates genuine transformation from religious feeling that never becomes real. Affections that never change how a person lives, however intense or sincerely felt, are not yet the thing Scripture is describing. But Edwards is equally insistent on the other side of the same claim: the sign is the *bent and habitual direction* of a life, not sinless or uninterrupted execution. A believer's practice, he writes, is like a needle drawn toward true north — it can wobble, be knocked off course by contrary wind, even seem to reverse for a season — and still be a needle that is, underneath the wobble, oriented toward the pole. The test was never whether the needle holds perfectly still. It was always which way it keeps returning.

That is the register this whole document is written in. What follows is not a standard to meet. It is what becomes visible, unevenly and over time, when a person's identity has actually been reorganized around what God says rather than what they perform, earn, or protect. Read it the way you would read a description of a tree's fruit — not as instructions for how to force the tree to produce, but as a way of recognizing what is actually growing when it does. *"Every healthy tree bears good fruit... a tree is known by its fruit"* (Mt 7:17, 20 ESV).

## Love — *Agapē*

Agapē is love that acts for another's good regardless of what it costs the one loving or what it earns in return — not affection as feeling, but affection as direction. Internally, this is what the Interior Life as Telos names "Loved and Knowing It": a person who no longer needs to extract love in order to have enough of it becomes free to give it without running a ledger.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In marriage and family**, love that keeps showing up on the hard days — not because the relationship is currently rewarding, but because the person has stopped needing it to be, in order to stay present in it.
- **In how resources are handled**, generosity that isn't calculated for return — money, time, and attention given to people who have no way to pay it back, and no discomfort in that fact.
- **Toward people who cannot benefit you**, the specific test John gives: *"Little children, let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth"* (1 Jn 3:18 ESV). Love that shows up as action toward someone with nothing to offer in exchange — not sentiment about them.
- **In conflict**, willingness to be inconvenienced by someone else's actual need, rather than performing care only where it's visible or costs nothing.

John makes the reverse test explicit and uncomfortable: *"If anyone says, 'I love God,' and hates his brother, he is a liar"* (1 Jn 4:20 ESV). Agapē toward God that produces no corresponding action toward people is not yet the thing this word names.

## Joy — *Chara*

Chara is gladness rooted in something that does not depend on circumstances resolving — not cheerfulness manufactured for the room, and not the absence of grief. Nehemiah names the connection directly: *"the joy of the LORD is your strength"* (Neh 8:10 ESV) — joy as a source of durability, not a decoration on top of it.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In suffering and adversity**, the capacity Paul names in his own imprisoned circumstances — *"I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content"* (Phil 4:11 ESV) — content without the circumstances having changed at all.
- **In ordinary work**, a person who can do unglamorous, repeated labor without needing it to be exciting in order to be worth doing — what Eugene Peterson, in *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* (1980), calls the discipleship of showing up faithfully in the same direction day after day, without the drama that makes obedience feel meaningful only when it's dramatic.
- **In community**, genuine capacity to rejoice at someone else's good news without a flicker of comparison or diminishment — Paul's *"rejoice with those who rejoice"* (Rom 12:15 ESV) actually functioning rather than being merely intended.
- **In loss**, grief that coexists with joy rather than being replaced by forced positivity — the same person who weeps can, in the same season, mean it when they say they are glad. Neither one is performed to manage how the moment looks to others.

## Peace — *Eirēnē*

Eirēnē is wholeness that holds under pressure — not the absence of conflict, but stability that doesn't require conflict's absence. This is the direct outward expression of what the Interior Life as Telos names "Settled": a groundedness that does not depend on outcomes falling right.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In conflict and offense**, the capacity to stay in a hard conversation without needing to win it, escape it, or manage the other person's opinion of you in order to feel okay when it ends.
- **In family systems under strain**, a non-anxious presence that doesn't require everyone else to be calm first — the kind of stability that actually lowers the temperature in a room rather than raising it.
- **In uncertainty about the future** — job loss, diagnosis, a decision with no clear right answer — the specific state Paul describes: *"do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds"* (Phil 4:6-7 ESV). Guarded, not achieved by the person's own effort to stop worrying.
- **In how disagreement is handled within community**, pursuing what actually resolves things rather than what merely avoids discomfort — Paul's "so then let us pursue what makes for peace and for mutual upbuilding" (Rom 14:19 ESV) as an active pursuit, not passive conflict-avoidance.

## Patience — *Makrothymia*

Makrothymia is literally "long-tempered" — the capacity to endure provocation, delay, or frustration without a short fuse. N.T. Wright's account in *After You Believe* (2010) of character formed by practice until it becomes second nature is especially apt here: patience under real provocation is almost never a single willpower decision in the moment. It is the accumulated shape of many prior small moments of not reacting, until not reacting is simply who the person now is.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **With people who are slow to change** — a spouse's persistent flaw, a child's repeated failure, a colleague's recurring mistake — patience that keeps engaging rather than writing the person off, matching Paul's own account of receiving mercy *"as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life"* (1 Tim 1:16 ESV) precisely because Christ was patient with him first.
- **In work that doesn't pay off quickly**, sustained effort toward something whose results are not yet visible, without needing an early win to keep going.
- **Under real provocation** — being interrupted, dismissed, or treated unfairly — a measured response that doesn't require the provoking person to apologize first in order for the patient person to stay steady.
- **In parenting or mentoring**, the capacity to say the same true thing calmly for the hundredth time without the tone curdling into contempt.

## Kindness — *Chrēstotēs*

Chrēstotēs is a disposition toward others that is actively useful and good to be around — not niceness as conflict-avoidance, but a settled bent toward doing others good. Paul's imperative is characteristically concrete: *"Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you"* (Eph 4:32 ESV) — kindness explicitly grounded in having received it first, not manufactured from personal warmth.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **Toward people who have wronged you**, kindness that doesn't wait for the offender to earn it back — the same forgiveness-shaped kindness Paul names as flowing directly from having been forgiven, not from the offender's remorse.
- **In everyday interactions with people who have nothing to offer** — a server, a stranger, someone lower in a hierarchy than you — a consistent quality of treatment that doesn't shift based on what the person can do for you.
- **In how correction is delivered**, kindness that can still tell someone a hard truth (see Gentleness, below, for the fuller treatment) rather than kindness that only shows up when nothing difficult needs to be said.
- **In the small, repeated choices of a day** — James K. A. Smith's account in *You Are What You Love* (2016) of habits training what a person actually loves applies directly here: kindness as a genuine outcome is rarely one dramatic act. It is what a person's ordinary, repeated small choices have trained them to default to when no one is grading the moment.

## Goodness — *Agathōsynē*

Agathōsynē is active moral excellence — goodness that does something, not merely goodness as the absence of wrongdoing. Colossians grounds this in identity directly: *"Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience"* (Col 3:12 ESV) — the imperative to "put on" a specific, active pattern flowing from an identity already given ("God's chosen ones, holy and beloved"), not from an attempt to earn that identity.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In vocation**, work done with integrity when no one is checking — Ephesians' specific example is instructive: *"Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need"* (Eph 4:28 ESV). Not merely stopping the wrong behavior, but redirecting the same energy toward honest labor that produces something to give away.
- **In speech**, words that actively build others up rather than merely avoiding cruelty — *"Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear"* (Eph 4:29 ESV).
- **In how power or advantage is used**, actively working for someone else's benefit when you have leverage and no one would notice if you didn't — the specific shape of "put on" goodness rather than passive non-harm.
- **In visible public life**, Jesus' own framing of the purpose of visible good works: *"let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven"* (Mt 5:16 ESV) — goodness aimed at directing attention toward God, not toward the person doing the good.

## Faithfulness — *Pistis*

Pistis here is reliability — being someone whose word and pattern of life can actually be trusted over time, not a single act of belief but a sustained, dependable character. Eugene Peterson's title itself names this outcome: *a long obedience in the same direction* — faithfulness measured in years and ordinary repetition, not in a single dramatic act of devotion.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In commitments**, doing what was said would be done, on the unglamorous days as much as the meaningful ones — a marriage, a friendship, a work commitment that holds when it has stopped being interesting.
- **In stewardship of what's been entrusted**, the parable of the talents' commendation — *"Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little; I will set you over much"* (Mt 25:21 ESV) — faithfulness with small, unremarkable responsibilities as the actual proving ground, not a substitute for it.
- **Across a long season of unanswered prayer or unresolved suffering**, continuing to show up in relationship with God and others without the relationship having produced the resolution being asked for.
- **In how a person is described by others over years**, not by a single testimony of transformation but by an accumulated reputation for reliability — the specific kind of evidence Edwards points to when he insists genuine affections are known by *sustained* practice, not a peak experience.

## Gentleness — *Prautēs*

Prautēs is strength under control — power that has chosen restraint, not weakness that has no other option. This is the direct outward expression of what the Interior Life as Telos names "Truthful Without Cruelty": the capacity to see and speak clearly without either weaponizing the truth or softening it into nothing.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In correction and conflict**, Paul's specific instruction: *"if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritually strong should restore him in a spirit of gentleness"* (Gal 6:1 ESV) — confronting the actual problem while treating the person with care, not choosing between honesty and kindness.
- **Toward people who are difficult or hostile**, a measured, non-defensive response that doesn't require winning or being right in order to stay steady — strength that has nothing to prove.
- **In positions of real authority or advantage**, restraint that doesn't use available power just because it's available — the same quality Jesus names of himself: *"I am gentle and lowly in heart"* (Mt 11:29 ESV), spoken by someone who, the text is careful to establish elsewhere, had every actual authority to claim otherwise.
- **In how criticism is received**, the capacity to hear a hard truth about yourself without either collapsing into shame or hardening into defensiveness — receiving correction the same way gentleness is meant to give it.

## Self-Control — *Enkrateia*

Enkrateia is mastery over one's own impulses, appetites, and reactions — not suppression, but genuine governance from a center that is no longer at the mercy of whatever wants to run the moment. N.T. Wright's account of practiced virtue becoming second nature applies with particular force here: self-control that has to be white-knuckled every single time is not yet the fruit Paul is naming; it is the effortful precursor to it.

What this produces when it meets an actual life:

- **In appetite and consumption** — food, alcohol, screens, spending — governed use rather than either compulsive indulgence or anxious restriction, the difference Paul draws between *"all things are lawful for me," but not all things are helpful. "All things are lawful for me," but I will not be dominated by anything* (1 Cor 6:12 ESV).
- **In speech under provocation**, the pause between being provoked and responding — James' framing: *"let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger"* (Jas 1:19 ESV) — a real, functioning gap where reaction used to be automatic.
- **In sexual integrity**, the capacity Paul names directly for believers navigating desire: *"each one of you know how to control his own body in holiness and honor"* (1 Thess 4:4 ESV) — governance, not merely the absence of opportunity to fail.
- **Under stress or threat**, a person whose worst moments are noticeably less volatile than they used to be — not because the pressure decreased, but because what used to run the room from underneath no longer has the same grip.

## A Note for the Practitioner

Everything above describes what becomes visible when the work of identity formation is bearing fruit. None of it is a goal to hand a client, a rubric to score them against, or evidence you are entitled to go looking for as proof the coaching is "working." James is emphatic that faith produces visible action — *"faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead"* (Jas 2:17 ESV) — but that claim describes what real faith does, not a test you administer to determine whether someone's faith is real. Those are two different uses of the same true sentence, and only one of them belongs in this room.

This document carries a specific risk the Interior Life as Telos document, being entirely interior-facing, does not carry to the same degree: because the outcomes named here are behavioral and observable, they are far easier to turn into a compliance checklist than a felt interior quality is. Resist that pull actively. If you notice yourself privately auditing a client against this list — tracking whether they're "showing" enough patience, enough generosity, enough self-control — you have started using a portrait of grace as an instrument of law, which is precisely the reversal this entire model exists to undo. The client's worth, standing, and belovedness were never contingent on producing any of this. What's described here is what tends to follow when that truth has actually landed, not what has to be produced in order to make it land.

Hold this the way Edwards himself held it: not as a snapshot you check a person against in a single sitting, but as the *direction* their practice is bent toward over time, wobble and all. A client who is visibly still failing in one domain while showing real, unmistakable fruit in another is not a mixed result. That is what a genuine, ongoing work of the Spirit in a real, still-being-sanctified person actually looks like. Your job is not to grade the current state against this picture. It is to know what you are tending toward, so that when you see even a small, uneven, genuine shoot of it, you recognize it for what it is.
