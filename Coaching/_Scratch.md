**Note � 2026-08-07 � Misguided Affections as Idolatry**

Practice must be clear on the connection between misguided, disorganized, controlling affections and idolatry. Society lacks clarity on this; clients may experience this truth as a surprise. Andrew will need to be able to defend it in-session.

### Core Claim
Misguided affections can become a form of idolatry even with no physical idol involved. Scripture presents idolatry as a matter of the heart's deepest loyalty, trust, and love � not merely the worship of carved images. Whatever occupies the place that belongs to God alone can function as an idol.

### Scriptural Anchors (ESV)
- Exodus 20:3 � "You shall have no other gods before me."
- Matthew 22:37 � "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind."
- Matthew 6:21 � "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."
- Colossians 3:5 � "Covetousness... is idolatry." Paul names an inward, excessive desire itself as idolatrous because it elevates something created into an ultimate object of longing.
- Prophetic literature (Israel's idolatry as spiritual adultery) � the heart is the real battleground; idolatry described as a turning-away of the heart, not merely external ritual violation.

### What Makes an Affection "Misguided"
Affections themselves are not wrong (love for God, neighbor, family, justice, beauty, truth are all commended). They become idol-like when:
- **disordered** � loving a good thing more than God
- **misdirected** � placing ultimate hope in something created rather than the Creator
- **controlling** � allowing a desire to govern decisions more than God's will

Examples: loving a relationship so much you compromise convictions to preserve it; loving success so much your identity depends on achievement; loving comfort so much obedience becomes secondary; loving approval so much fear of people outweighs fear of God. None of these things are sinful in themselves � they become idol-like when they assume God's place.

### Diagnostic Question (client-usable)
"What do I believe I must have in order to be whole, secure, or truly happy?" If the answer is ultimately anything other than God, that thing may be functioning as an idol of the heart.

### Key Distinction
- Receiving God's gifts with gratitude, vs.
- Depending on those gifts to do what only God can do.

### Positive Vision (not suppression)
The biblical call is not to suppress affection but to rightly order it. When love for God is primary, other loves find their proper place � relationships, work, possessions, ambitions can be enjoyed as gifts rather than worshiped as gods. The concern is less about the intensity of affection than its object and its place in the hierarchy of the heart's loves.

**Note — 2026-08-07**

### The Kind of Person Who Has a Felt Knowing of Their True Covenant Identity

This describes the formation *endpoint* CIC is aimed at, not a personality type — it is what it looks like when a client's felt, functional, lived experience has caught up to a positional identity that was already true from new birth (see Practice Definition, "Positional Identity vs. Identity Formation"). Two people can hold the identical correct doctrine — "I am a new creation, adopted, secure in Christ" — and differ completely on this axis: one has it as a fact they can defend in an argument, the other has it as the ground they are already standing on before the argument starts. The description below is of the second person.

**1. The identity functions as a starting point, not a conclusion.**
They don't have to search for evidence, recall a verse, or reason their way into "I am beloved and secure" before it's operative — it is the lens events pass through, not a position they arrive at after reflection. This is the difference between a native speaker and someone translating in their head: the native speaker isn't faster at retrieving the right words, they're not retrieving at all. Someone with only cognitive knowledge of their identity has to remember it under pressure; someone with felt knowing has to work to forget it.

**2. The identity holds under contact with failure, not just in curated conditions.**
Per Nouwen's "Broken" movement (*Life of the Beloved*), an identity that only holds when things are going well isn't the true self showing up — it's a mood. This person's sense of who they are doesn't collapse, need repair, or go into hiding when they fail, sin, or are criticized. They can look directly at their own weakness without shame-collapse and without minimizing it, because the security of the identity was never contingent on performance in the first place — there is nothing to protect it from. This is the practical test a coach can actually observe: what happens to this person's sense of self in the fifteen minutes after they've been exposed at their worst.

**3. The identity was received, not earned — so external validation doesn't govern it.**
They don't need approval, achievement, or being needed to feel like themselves, because the identity was never built out of those inputs to begin with (Rom. 6:11's *logizomai* — reckoning with what is already true, not manifesting what is not yet true). Withheld praise or someone else's disapproval can still hurt, but it doesn't destabilize *who they take themselves to be* the way it does for someone whose sense of self is still under construction from external material.

**4. The identity is given away, not hoarded.**
Per Nouwen's "Given" movement, belovedness that isn't extended outward curdles into self-focus. Because this person's security isn't a scarce resource they have to defend, they aren't threatened by someone else's belovedness, competence, or standing — they can actually see and affirm it in another person without it costing them anything.

**Distinguishing marker for coaching diagnosis:** the gap this describes is a *formation* gap, not a deficiency in the client's actual standing — nothing is missing in their position, only in how far their conscious self-understanding, affections, and will have caught up to it (Rom. 12:2; Eph. 4:22–24). A client who cognitively affirms their identity but doesn't feel it is not further from God than this person — they are at an earlier point in the same already-guaranteed process, not on a different track.

**Note — 2026-08-07**

### Q: False identity pattern vs. an implicit process operating according to falsehood, disordered affections, false covenant objects, character wounds

These are not two competing diagnostic targets. One names a **content node**; the other names the **register** — the plane on which that whole causal chain actually runs. They answer different questions.

**False identity pattern (= false identity structure, per CIC terminology)** is a *what*. It is the specific, nameable behavioral and relational system a person has built to protect and serve a specific false covenant object — the operating self organized around securing that trust. It is coherent and predictable, not random, because every part of it exists to defend one thing. It is the output that gets diagnosed and named at Stage 2 (e.g., "I am significant when I am producing more than expected; if I stop, I become replaceable and therefore worthless").

**Character wound → disordered affections → false covenant object → false identity pattern** is the *causal chain* that produces that output. In sequence: a wound (betrayal, terror, or ambivalence) damages the heart's trust in God as a reliable covenant partner for a specific need → the affections (the heart's settled trust/desire/fear commitments, not emotions) reorganize around a substitute → that substitute becomes the false covenant object (what the heart actually trusts in God's place) → the false identity pattern is built to protect and serve that object.

**"Implicit process"** is neither a fifth node in that chain nor a synonym for any one of its four terms. It names the *register* the whole chain operates on — procedural, felt, pre-verbal, encoded before or beneath conscious/propositional awareness (Curt Thompson/Siegel's implicit vs. explicit memory systems, load-bearing in the CIC model). The wound, the disordered affections, the false covenant object, and the false identity pattern all run at this implicit level, which is precisely why a person can hold correct explicit theology ("God loves me," "my worth is in Christ") while the implicit architecture still runs the old organizing pattern under pressure — the gap "I know it in my head but not my heart" describes. Explicit belief change that never reaches the implicit level does not persist under threat.

**The distinction that matters in practice:** naming the false identity pattern answers "what has the heart built, and what is it protecting?" Naming something as "implicit" answers a different question — "at what level is this actually operating, and why didn't stating the correct belief change it?" Collapsing the two — treating "implicit process" as if it were itself the diagnostic target, rather than the level at which the four content terms operate — is a version of the same misdiagnosis pitfall CIC flags elsewhere: naming the register instead of reaching the organizing center the register is running.

*Source: [[Covenant Identity — Disordered Affections and the Diagnostic Entry — Mechanism]]; [[Covenant Identity — False Covenant Objects & False Identity Structures — Terms Reference]]; [[Covenant Identity — Terminology & Language Reference]] (Curt Thompson/Siegel implicit vs. explicit memory).*

**Note — 2026-08-07 — Root vs. Outcome: Implicit Processes/Schemas as Root, Disordered Affections as Outcome**

Flag: not sure this root-to-outcome connection is stated clearly anywhere in current documentation. Needs a documentation check and, if missing, an explicit statement somewhere load-bearing (Practice Definition or Mechanism doc).

### Core Claim
The practice recognizes that implicit processes and schemas are the *root* — misguided, disorganized, controlling affections are the *outcome* they produce, not a separate or parallel problem. This is a causal/directional claim: implicit process/schema → disordered affection. If the model treats these as two co-equal or loosely-related problem areas instead of root-and-outcome, diagnosis and intervention will misfire (working on the affection directly without addressing the schema generating it, or vice versa in the wrong order).

### Relation to existing scratch note above (2026-08-07, "False identity pattern vs. implicit process")
That note already established the causal chain: character wound → disordered affections → false covenant object → false identity pattern, all running on the implicit register. This note sharpens one link in that chain specifically: the *schema/implicit process itself* — not just the wound — is what should be named as the root that generates the disordered affection, not the affection treated as the starting point of analysis.

### Why this needs explicit documentation
- Affects diagnostic sequencing: a coach who treats disordered affection as the root will intervene at the wrong level (arguing with the affection or its behavioral output) rather than surfacing and addressing the implicit schema generating it.
- Affects how the model is defended in-session and to outside reviewers — "why do we work with implicit process instead of just addressing the feeling/behavior" needs a clean, citable answer.
- Should be checked against [[Covenant Identity — Disordered Affections and the Diagnostic Entry — Mechanism]] and the CIC Model Foundation docs to confirm whether this is implicit-but-assumed or genuinely undocumented.
