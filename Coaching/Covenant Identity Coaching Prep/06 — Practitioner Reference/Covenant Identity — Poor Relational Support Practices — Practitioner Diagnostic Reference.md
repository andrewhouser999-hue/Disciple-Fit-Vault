# Poor Relational Support Practices — Practitioner Diagnostic Reference

## Purpose

This document catalogs how well-meaning people — including well-meaning Christians — fail to provide real emotional, psychological, and relational support to someone who is struggling or suffering, even while intending to help. It exists for two paired uses, deliberately shared across two tracks by Andrew's direction:

1. **CIC practitioner use** — recognizing these patterns in a client's relational history (why past support attempts from family, friends, or church community failed to land, or actively wounded), and in the coach's own reflexes (self-supervision, countertransference watch).
2. **Beholding Together leader training** — teaching lay small-group leaders what *not* to do when a group member discloses pain, so the group's paired/triad practice sessions don't reproduce the very failures the curriculum exists to correct.

**Scope note on track separation:** [[feedback_practice_separation]] normally keeps CIC and Formation & Transition content from blending. This document is a deliberate, Andrew-authorized exception — the two audiences need the identical content for the identical reason (recognizing bad support so as not to repeat it), and the list itself carries no CIC-specific vocabulary, diagnostic tool, or theological architecture. It is written to stand on its own for a lay leader with no CIC background.

**What this is not:** a clinical taxonomy, a checklist to hand to a struggling person, or a basis for pathologizing anyone's sincere attempt to help. Nearly everyone does several of these things by default — the point is recognition, not condemnation.

## Cluster A — The Righting Reflex (Fixing Before Understanding)

**1. Premature problem-solving.** Jumping to solutions, action steps, or "have you tried…" before the person has finished being heard. Sourced to Miller & Rollnick's *righting reflex* — the helper's own anxiety at witnessing pain converts into an urge to resolve it, which communicates that the person's distress is a problem to be managed rather than a reality to be accompanied.

**2. Unsolicited advice.** Advice given before it is asked for, especially in the first telling of a hard thing. Distinct from #1 in that the advice may be sound — the failure is sequencing: it forecloses the person's own processing and implies the helper already knows what they need.

**3. Reflexive silver-lining.** "At least…" statements ("at least you still have your health," "at least it wasn't worse") that redirect attention from the loss to a compensating positive before the loss has been acknowledged. Functions as minimization dressed as encouragement.

**4. Toxic positivity.** Insisting on a positive frame — "stay positive," "it'll all work out" — as a response to disclosed pain, rather than after genuine acknowledgment. Differs from #3 in being a blanket demand for a feeling state rather than a specific reframe; it teaches the person that their actual emotional state is unwelcome in this relationship.

**5. Certainty-under-ambiguity.** Needing to resolve the "why" question when the suffering is genuinely unresolved or mysterious — offering a confident explanation (divine purpose, karmic cause, life lesson) the helper cannot actually know, in order to relieve the helper's own discomfort with not knowing. Job's three friends are the biblical case study: each offers a coherent, confident explanation for Job's suffering, and God's verdict (Job 42:7) is that they "have not spoken of me what is right."

## Cluster B — Theological/Spiritual Bypassing (Christian-Specific)

**6. Job's-friends pattern.** Assuming or implying that suffering is evidence of hidden sin, insufficient faith, or unconfessed wrongdoing, and offering that as the explanation for someone's pain. This is the named biblical failure mode, not a design-judgment label — see the full exegesis in [[Covenant Identity — Healing from Emotional Trauma — Biblical Research]], which treats Job as "the control case against premature explanation."

**7. Spiritual bypassing.** Using spiritual language, practice, or belief to avoid direct contact with a painful feeling — one's own or another's. Sourced to Welwood, already active in the CIC evidence base via the Renee K. worked example. Distinct from #6 in scope: bypassing doesn't require blaming the sufferer, only skipping past the feeling toward the spiritual frame.

**8. "God won't give you more than you can handle."** A specific, frequently misapplied cliché (it misreads 1 Corinthians 10:13, which concerns temptation, not suffering broadly) that functions as a conversation-stopper — it tells the person their pain has already been sized and approved by God, closing the door on lament.

**9. Premature Romans 8:28 / "everything happens for a reason."** Deploying a true doctrinal claim (God works all things for good) at a moment when the person needs to be heard in their pain, not reassured about its ultimate resolution. The doctrine isn't false; the timing makes it function as an exit from the conversation rather than truth offered into it.

**10. "I'll pray for you" as a closing move.** Using prayer as the terminal act of a conversation about someone's suffering rather than pairing it with concrete presence or action — the biblical warrant for the failure mode is James 2:15–16 (wishing someone well while withholding what they actually need).

**11. Premature calls to gratitude or trust.** "You should be more grateful," "just trust God more" — language that turns the sufferer's emotional state into a spiritual deficiency requiring correction, rather than receiving the state as legitimate data about their experience.

**12. Shaming the emotion, especially anger at God.** Treating lament, doubt, or anger directed at God as itself sinful or spiritually immature, rather than as a biblically sanctioned form of address (the psalms of lament, Job's own protests, which God does not rebuke). This is a standing caution already tracked in [[feedback_covenant_authority_lament_caution]] — any framework emphasizing God's unchallengeable authority must preserve Scripture's own sanctioned space for lament and protest.

## Cluster C — Withdrawal and Inconsistent Presence

**13. Avoidance.** Physically or conversationally disappearing when a relationship becomes emotionally demanding — not returning calls, changing the subject, finding reasons not to visit. Often driven by the helper's own unmanaged discomfort with proximity to pain, not by any judgment about the sufferer.

**14. Crisis-only support.** Showing up hard during the acute phase (the funeral, the diagnosis week — meals, cards, visits) and then largely disappearing once the acute phase ends, missing the long tail of grief or struggle that typically outlasts the community's attention span by months or years.

**15. Conditional presence.** Support that continues only if the person is perceived to be "recovering correctly" or on the helper's expected timeline, and quietly withdraws if they aren't — communicating that the relationship was contingent on a performance, not unconditional.

**16. Surface-level check-ins.** Asking "how are you?" as a social script that doesn't actually make room for a true answer — asked in passing, without follow-up capacity, such that an honest response would create more discomfort than the asker was prepared for.

## Cluster D — Self-Centering the Support

**17. Comparison / narrative hijacking.** "I know exactly how you feel — when I…" followed by an extended account of the helper's own similar experience, which redirects attention from the sufferer to the helper and implicitly claims equivalence the helper cannot actually establish.

**18. Making it about the helper's own distress.** Becoming visibly upset, needing comfort, or requiring the sufferer to manage the helper's emotional reaction to their pain — a form of reverse caretaking that adds a relational burden onto the person who is already suffering. This is the direct behavioral failure of what Friedman calls *non-anxious presence*: the helper's own anxiety becomes the dominant signal in the room instead of a stable, regulated presence the sufferer can lean on.

**19. Curiosity without care.** Asking probing questions to satisfy the helper's own interest in the details of what happened, rather than to serve the sufferer's need to be known — often detectable because the questions continue past the point where they'd help the sufferer and start resembling gossip-gathering.

**20. Triangulation.** Discussing the sufferer's situation with third parties instead of directly with the person, including under the cover of a "prayer request" shared without permission — a breach of trust structurally identical to gossip even when the stated motive is care.

**21. Rescue/savior posture.** Offering help in a way that requires being needed, thanked, or credited — support that is subtly oriented around the helper's own sense of usefulness or identity as a helper, rather than around what the sufferer actually needs, and which can curdle into resentment or withdrawal if the sufferer doesn't respond with sufficient gratitude.

**22. Performative support.** Public displays of care (social media posts, group announcements) that substitute for or outpace actual private presence — visible concern without a corresponding private relationship behind it.

## Cluster E — Boundary and Regulation Failures

**23. Over-identification / merging.** Absorbing the sufferer's pain so completely that the helper becomes dysregulated themselves, which — paired with #18 — requires the sufferer to now manage two people's distress instead of one.

**24. Under-identification / clinical detachment.** The opposite failure: responding with such flatness or procedural distance that the sufferer experiences the interaction as processing rather than presence — technically correct responses delivered without warmth.

**25. Weaponized vulnerability.** Using something disclosed in a moment of trust or support later, in a different context, as leverage for correction or rebuke ("well, you did say you struggle with…") — this converts a support relationship into a surveillance relationship retroactively and reliably ends future disclosure.

**26. Confidentiality breach.** Sharing what was disclosed in confidence, with anyone, for any stated reason (including "so we can pray for them together") without the person's explicit permission.

---

## Cluster F — Timeline and Pace Violations

**27. Timeline pressure.** "Isn't it time to move on," "you should be over this by now" — imposing an external schedule on a process (grief, recovery, healing) that does not run on a fixed or predictable timeline.

**28. Impatience with non-linear healing.** Frustration, subtle withdrawal, or visible disappointment when someone who seemed to be improving relapses into acute grief or struggle — treating healing as a line that should only move forward rather than the recursive process it actually is.

**29. Colonizing the narrative.** Deciding, and stating, what the person's suffering *means* or *is for* — even with good theological intent — rather than accompanying the person as they arrive at their own meaning on their own timeline. Distinct from #6 and #9 in that the "meaning" offered need not be blame or a cliché; it can be a sophisticated theological interpretation that is still premature and still not the sufferer's own.

**30. Urgency mismatch.** Treating the sufferer's pace of disclosure or need for repetition (telling the same story multiple times, needing to revisit the same pain) as an obstacle to the helper's own comfort, signaled by subtle cues to hurry up, wrap up, or move to a lighter topic.

## How This List Functions in Each Track

**CIC use:** Cross-reference against [[Covenant Identity — Self-Supervision Template]] (Presence Quality and Avoidance Inventory sections already probe several of these reflexes in the coach) and against a client's relational-history intake — when a client describes past support that didn't help, this list gives the coach precise, non-clinical vocabulary for what happened to them, without requiring a diagnosis of the people who failed them.

**Beholding Together use:** Cross-reference against [[Beholding Together — Leader Readiness Rubric]] (regulation under others' distress, holding without fixing) and [[Beholding Together — In-Session Stop-Redirect Protocol]] — a leader who recognizes Cluster A (the righting reflex) and Cluster E (boundary failures) in themselves before they happen is the direct mechanism by which the curriculum's four-element model (awareness, safety, beholding, repetition) survives contact with a live, untrained small group.

**Common failure signature across both tracks:** nearly every item above is a version of the same underlying move — relieving the *helper's* discomfort (with silence, with not knowing, with proximity to pain, with someone else's pace) rather than staying present to the *sufferer's* actual experience. Naming that common root is more useful for training than memorizing thirty items: the diagnostic question in the moment is always "whose discomfort am I actually managing right now?"
