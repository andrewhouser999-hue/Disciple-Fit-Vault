# Covenant Identity — Faith as a Mustard Seed (Matthew 17:20) — Practitioner Exegetical Reference

> *"He said to them, 'Because of your little faith. For truly, I say to you, if you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, "Move from here to there," and it will move, and nothing will be impossible for you.'"*
> — Matthew 17:20 (ESV)

---

## Document Purpose

This document corrects a widely-held misreading of the mustard-seed faith saying (Matt 17:20; cf. Luke 17:6) that surfaces regularly in clients pursuing faith-based transformation: the assumption that faith operates on a **quantity threshold** — that the coaching or spiritual-growth task is to generate *more* faith-intensity, and that insufficient results mean insufficient believing. The popular reading inverts the grammar of the passage and, left uncorrected, functions as a shame-generating performance metric layered onto a client's relationship with God.

Primary placement within the model: biblical anchor for **correcting a false covenant object** — "my own certainty/faith-intensity" functioning as what the client believes mediates God's action, displacing trust in God's character as the actual mediator. Directly relevant wherever a client's presenting language includes some version of "I just don't have enough faith."

Secondary placement: cross-reference for [[Covenant Identity — Faith, Certainty & the Reckoning Pattern]], which already flags the adjacent Word-of-Faith/"certainty as technique" proximity risk around Mark 11:22-24. This document supplies the parallel exegetical guardrail for the mustard-seed form of the same distortion.

---

## 1. Text & Context

**Matthew 17:20** sits immediately after a failed exorcism at the foot of the Mount of Transfiguration (17:14-19). The disciples could not cast a demon out of a boy; they ask Jesus privately why. Jesus answers, "Because of your little faith [ὀλιγοπιστίαν, *oligopistian*]," then gives the mustard-seed saying.

**Textual variant note:** Many manuscripts append a verse 21 — "But this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting" — almost certainly a later scribal harmonization imported from Mark 9:29. It is absent from the earliest and best witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) and bracketed or omitted in critical editions (ESV footnotes it). It should not be treated as part of Matthew's original text or built into client-facing teaching.

**Luke 17:6** carries the identical core saying in a *different* narrative setting: apostles asking Jesus to "increase our faith" (17:5) after His hard teaching on repeated forgiveness (17:1-4). Luke's application is about the capacity to forgive repeatedly, not miracle-working power over illness — a meaningfully different use of the same proverbial image.

**Form-critical note:** Mark 11:22-23 carries closely related "mountain-moving" language in yet a third setting (the cursed fig tree / temple confrontation) *without* the mustard-seed comparison. The saying appears to have functioned as a somewhat free-floating teaching that each Gospel writer anchored to a different occasion — a caution against building too much theological weight on any one narrative context as though the saying were coined specifically for it.

## 2. Language — ὡς and the Comparison Structure

### The lexical range of ὡς (*hōs*)

Standard lexicons (Thayer, Louw-Nida, Friberg) gloss ὡς as "as, like, even as" — a comparative particle/adverb. This is correct but incomplete for exegetical purposes: **ὡς is grammatically indifferent to *which* property of the compared item is in view.** It marks resemblance without specifying whether the resemblance is one of size, kind, function, or potential. That has to be supplied by context — by what property of the compared noun is culturally or narratively salient — not by ὡς itself.

### What ὡς is actually attached to

ἐὰν ἔχητε πίστιν **ὡς κόκκον** σινάπεως — "if you have faith as **a grain/seed** of mustard." κόκκον (accusative singular, "grain" or "seed") is a discrete, pre-growth object — a seed in the hand, not a tree. There is no growth verb (αὐξάνω) and no tree-noun (δένδρον) anywhere in Matthew 17:20 or Luke 17:6. Compare Matthew 13:32, where the growth trajectory is spelled out with its own verb: "smaller than all seeds, but **when it is grown** [αὐξηθῇ] ... becomes a tree ... birds ... come and make nests in its branches" (see §4). That apparatus — growth, tree, birds — is textually present in 13:31-32 and textually absent from 17:20. This is the honest grammatical constraint on any reading that imports tree/growth/impact imagery directly into 17:20: the comparison-object named in the verse is the seed itself, not its eventual growth.

### ἐὰν ἔχητε — grammatical mood

Third-class conditional (ἐάν + present subjunctive) — a hypothetical, not a statement that the disciples currently possess even mustard-seed-sized faith. Jesus is not commending an amount they have; He is positing a hypothetical threshold.

### ὀλιγοπιστίαν vs. κόκκον σινάπεως — the paradox that creates the interpretive crux

ὀλιγοπιστία ("little-faith-ness," a Matthean coinage — used only by this Gospel writer in the NT) is the diagnosis Jesus has just given the disciples. κόκκος σινάπεως is proverbially *smaller* than "little" — the standard idiom in the period for the smallest conceivable quantity (paralleled in rabbinic usage, e.g., measuring a minimal spot of blood "as small as a mustard seed"). Reading the verse as "even a size this small would be sufficient" creates a real coherence problem: it implies the disciples' actual "little" faith should have already been more than enough, which contradicts the diagnosis Jesus just delivered. This tension — not a minor translation quibble — is the actual center of the scholarly disagreement addressed in §5-6 below.

### κόκκον σινάπεως — no stated trait in the Greek itself

The Greek supplies no adjective, no size-qualifier, no growth-qualifier on the seed. "Faith **as small as** a mustard seed" (NIV, NLT) and "faith **as big as** a mustard seed" (GNB, NCV) are both translators supplying a trait the Greek does not state. The more literal renderings (KJV, RSV, NKJV, NASB — "faith as a grain of mustard seed") preserve the actual ambiguity rather than resolving it in translation.

## 3. Historical-Cultural Context

"Mountain-moving" and "tree-uprooting" language was stock rabbinic hyperbole for accomplishing something extraordinarily difficult. A sage skilled at resolving intricate legal or scriptural difficulties could be honored with the title *oqer harim* — "uprooter of mountains." First-century hearers likely received Jesus's saying as vivid hyperbolic idiom for "accomplishing the impossible," in the same rhetorical register as "swallowing a camel" (Matt 23:24) — not a literalistic claim about geology. This does not empty the saying of real content about faith and divine power; it clarifies what *kind* of claim is being made — rhetorical intensification of a genuine truth, not a mechanical formula for producing outcomes.

## 4. Intertextual Connections — The Two Mustard-Seed Sayings

The **Parable of the Mustard Seed (Matt 13:31-32)** uses the identical image for different theological work, and distinguishing the two is essential to avoiding the most common misreading:

> *"He put another parable before them, saying, 'The kingdom of heaven is like a grain of mustard seed that a man took and sowed in his field. It is the smallest of all seeds, but when it has grown it is larger than all the garden plants and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.'"* — Matthew 13:31-32 (ESV)

Here the mustard seed is the *kingdom of heaven* itself — the point is a paradoxical growth trajectory from insignificant beginning to a tree large enough to shelter birds, an image that deliberately echoes Daniel 4:12 and Ezekiel 17:23's pictures of a great kingdom sheltering the nations. This parable is *about* growth over time, told with a growth verb and full narrative.

**Matthew 17:20 is not about growth at all.** It is a single-moment comparison of quantity or quality (the interpretive question at issue), with no growth trajectory in view. Reading 17:20 through 13:31-32's lens — "even small faith grows into something big" — is an exegetically common move, but it imports the parable's growth-logic into a saying that does not itself contain that logic. If a synthesis of the two texts is wanted for teaching purposes, it should be presented explicitly as a canonical/thematic synthesis across two distinct sayings, not as what 17:20 says in isolation.

## 5. Theological Weight — The Interpretive Crux

There is genuine, unresolved scholarly disagreement over what Jesus is asserting in 17:20. Three live readings:

**(A) Quantity-sufficiency reading (popular/traditional).** Faith doesn't need to be large — even a minuscule but genuine amount is sufficient, because potency resides in the *object* of faith (God), not the psychological intensity of the believer. Theologically attractive and consistent with the broader biblical pattern of faith as a receptive instrument rather than a generative cause (Rom 4; Eph 2:8-9) — but does not resolve the ὀλιγοπιστία coherence problem in §2.

**(B) Potency/growth reading.** The mustard seed's salient trait is not its size but its capacity to produce something disproportionately great from something small — supported in current scholarship (see §6, Quaye 2024) and resolving the coherence problem by relocating the emphasis from *quantity of faith* to *kind/quality of faith*.

**(C) Ironic/rebuke reading.** Jesus is not commending small faith as adequate; He is exposing that the disciples do not even possess *that much*. The comparison to something proverbially smaller than "little faith" functions as a rhetorical indictment, not an encouragement. This also resolves the coherence problem — through irony rather than through redefining the compared trait.

(A) and (C) are not mutually exclusive; the saying can simultaneously relocate potency in God and function as a rebuke of present unbelief. (B) is a genuinely distinct move and the one with the most direct textual argumentation behind it (§6).

What the text does **not** support under any reading: the popular therapeutic-culture gloss "believe hard enough and you will get the outcome you want." That inverts the grammar (a third-class hypothetical, not an achieved condition already in the disciples' possession) and ignores the hyperbolic idiom (§3).

## 6. Interpretive Debate & Author Positions

**Ebenezer Quaye** (PhD, New Testament Studies; Senior Lecturer, Department of Theological Studies, Valley View University, Ghana) argues the (B) potency reading directly and at length in a 2024 peer-reviewed article devoted entirely to this problem:

> "If the Lord already rebuked the disciples for their littleness of faith, why would He again commend faith as small as [a] mustard seed as something worthwhile? ... this paper concludes that by the Lord's reference to the mustard seed ... He was referring to the **power or potential** of the seed, despite its small size, rather than focusing on the seed's smallness or size itself."
> — Quaye, E. (2024). "Resolving the Problem of the Expression 'Faith as Small as a Mustard Seed' in Matthew 17:20." *E-Journal of Humanities, Arts and Social Sciences* 5(14), 2480-2489. https://doi.org/10.38159/ehass.20245143

Quaye's method: a tenor/ground/vehicle simile analysis (Richards' rhetorical framework) establishing that the Greek text assigns **no salient qualifying trait** to the mustard seed at all — every English translation that reads "small as" or "big as" a mustard seed is supplying that trait in translation, not translating it from the source. He supports the potency reading by tracing the "inability" vocabulary running through the whole pericope (οὐκ ἠδυνήθησαν / v.16, οὐκ ἠδυνήθημεν / v.19 — both from δύναμαι, "ability"), citing Frederick Dale Bruner's *Matthew: A Commentary, Vol. 2: The Churchbook* (Word Books, 2004, p. 184) for the observation that inability is the pericope's unifying theme. **Caveat:** Quaye's own conclusion is general growth/potency language ("power or potential ... to produce great results"); he does not extend the argument to the more specific imagery of invasive roots or a tree sheltering "many" birds — that additional detail belongs to 13:31-32 alone (§4), and should be flagged as a synthesis across two texts if used, not attributed to 17:20's own wording.

**D.A. Carson** (in *An Introduction to the New Testament*, with Moo and Morris, cited by Quaye for pericope-structuring purposes, and independently in his *Matthew* commentary) takes an adjacent but more modest position — a "quality, not quantity" reading that stops short of the growth/potency argument:

> "It probably does not refer so much to the littleness of their faith as to its poverty.... Little faith, like a little mustard seed, can be effectual; poor faith, like that of the disciples' here, is ineffectual."

This reframes the operative contrast as *poverty/wrong-kind* (ineffectual, misdirected) versus *littleness/small-but-genuine* (effectual) — closer to (B) than to a pure size reading, but without Quaye's growth/potency emphasis.

**A third position** (the ironic/rebuke reading, (C) above) is associated in the broader commentary tradition with readings that treat the saying as *a fortiori* hyperbole rather than a redefinition of the compared trait — resolving the same coherence problem through irony instead of through reinterpreting what the mustard seed symbolizes. **Flagged honestly:** I was not able to verify a specific, citable page reference for this position in named commentaries (candidates include R.T. France and John Nolland, both cited in adjacent contexts by Quaye's bibliography) in this research session — if this document is used to make a specific attribution claim in future teaching material, that citation should be verified against the primary commentary before publication.

## 7. Coaching Translation

This text is a live correction point in transformation-focused coaching, because the popular "quantity of faith" misreading is one of the most common performance-based distortions clients bring into their relationship with God.

**Primary mechanism — affections/disordered worship.** A client operating from the quantity misreading is functionally worshiping his or her own certainty or emotional intensity as the mediator of God's action, rather than resting faith in God's character as the actual mediator. This is a subtle false covenant object — self-generated confidence substituting for trust in God — and correcting the grammar (a hypothetical, not an achieved threshold; potency located in the object of faith, not the subject's felt conviction) directly dismantles it.

**Spiritual Formation layer.** Rightly read, the saying is *anti*-performance. It does not ask for more faith-effort; on the (C) ironic reading it exposes that the disciples' actual posture in the moment was not trust at all (fear, self-reliance, perhaps shame after a public failure — 17:14). On the (B) potency reading, it relocates what matters from measurable intensity to whether the faith present is *genuine and rightly directed*, however initially small. Either way, formation work here is "notice what was actually trusted in the moment instead of God" — an examen-shaped move — not "try harder to believe."

**False identity structure.** Watch for the implicit belief "my faith isn't big enough, therefore I am spiritually deficient or disqualified" — a shame-based identity distortion masquerading as humility. This text, correctly exegeted, directly contradicts that belief: the issue was never the *size* threshold the disciples needed to reach; it was *what/whom* their trust actually rested on.

**Subordinate psychological layer.** Maps onto Kegan-Lahey's competing-commitment structure: stated goal ("I want to trust God for X") alongside a hidden competing commitment ("I need to feel certain before I act/trust"), with a big assumption underneath ("uncertainty means I don't really have faith"). Naming that ladder can make the correction land experientially, not just cognitively.

**What the client needs to feel, not just know:** relief that the bar was never "generate enough confident feeling," and grief/release for shame already accumulated around "not having enough faith" as a personal deficiency verdict.

## 8. Session Application

- **Diagnostic use.** Strong entry point for surfacing a false covenant object where "my own certainty/faith-feeling" is functioning as what the client trusts, rather than God's character. Probe: *"When you say you don't have enough faith — enough for what, and what do you believe faith-quantity is supposed to produce?"* Often exposes a hidden bargain ("if I believe hard enough, God owes me the outcome") — cross-reference [[Covenant Identity — Faith, Certainty & the Reckoning Pattern]] where this same bargain appears in Word-of-Faith/certainty-as-technique form.
- **Formation use.** Between-session practice: not a faith-intensity audit, but an examen turned toward the disciples' own question — "why couldn't I...?" — asking what was actually leaned on in a hard moment instead of God (control, performance, withdrawal).
- **Declarative / Lie Eliminator use.** Candidate lie: *"My faith has to be big enough before God will act,"* or *"Doubt disqualifies me."* Counter-declaration: faith's power was never in its size — it is in the God it is aimed at.
- **Framework anchor.** Useful contrast pairing with Matt 13:31-32 (§4) if teaching both texts together — one is about the Kingdom's *trajectory* (small beginning, cosmic end), the other about *where potency lives in the present moment* (not in the believer's felt certainty). Keep them explicitly distinct in any teaching material to avoid the common conflation this document exists to correct.
