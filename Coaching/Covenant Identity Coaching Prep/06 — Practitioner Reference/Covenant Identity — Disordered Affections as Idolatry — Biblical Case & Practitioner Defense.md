# Disordered Affections as Idolatry — The Biblical Case & Practitioner Defense

*What this document is:* The direct theological case, stated at the level a practitioner can use to defend it under client or peer challenge, that misguided, disordered, or controlling affections constitute a form of idolatry even when no physical idol is present. This is a *general* claim — distinct from and prior to the *domain-specific functional-exclusion* diagnostic in [[Covenant Identity — God-Exclusion Map]], which explicitly brackets general idolatry to do narrower diagnostic work. This document supplies the broader claim that document assumes but does not itself argue.

**Why this document exists:** Andrew identified a real gap — the practice needs to be unambiguous about the affections/idolatry connection, because clients will not arrive with this framework already in place, may experience it as a surprising or even unwelcome reframe of "just really loving someone/something," and Andrew needs a defensible, source-anchored answer ready rather than an improvised one when the claim is challenged.

**Who uses it:** Practitioner only. Not client-facing as written — see *Client-Facing Translation* below for how to bring the concept into session without the apparatus.

---

## The Core Claim

Misguided affections can become a form of idolatry even without a physical idol involved. Scripture treats idolatry as a matter of the heart's deepest loyalty, trust, and love — not merely the worship of carved images. Whatever occupies the place that belongs to God alone functions as an idol, regardless of whether it is ever consciously worshipped as one.

This is the claim in its strongest, most general form. It is stronger than — and the foundation underneath — the functional-exclusion diagnostic already built into this model. Where the God-Exclusion Map asks the narrower diagnostic question "in which domains is God absent from the operating picture," this document answers the prior question a skeptical client or peer will actually ask: *why call this idolatry at all, when I haven't rejected God or built an altar to anything?*

## The Scriptural Case

**Exodus 20:3 (ESV)** — "You shall have no other gods before me." The first commandment does not merely forbid worshiping named false gods (Baal, Asherah, the gods of Egypt). It establishes a structural principle that outlasts its original polytheistic context: God claims the position of ultimate loyalty, and anything else placed in that position — named god or not — violates the command. The commandment's force does not depend on the substitute being religiously labeled.

**Matthew 22:37 (ESV)** — "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind." Jesus names this the greatest commandment. Its structure is totalizing: *all* heart, *all* soul, *all* mind. A love command stated this way defines its own violation — not the presence of competing loves (Scripture commands loving neighbor, spouse, and children), but a competing love that consistently receives the heart's *highest* love, *ultimate* trust, or *defining* devotion, such that it begins to occupy ground the command reserves for God alone.

**Matthew 6:21 (ESV)** — "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." This is the diagnostic mechanism, not just a moral claim. Treasure is not identified by profession but by where the heart actually locates itself — an empirical, observable pattern (time, energy, anxiety, attention) rather than a self-report. This is why the claim is defensible in session: it does not require accusing the client of *believing* something false. It only requires observing where their heart has, in practice, gone to rest.

**Colossians 3:5 (ESV)** — "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry." This is the single most direct text for the claim. Paul does not say covetousness *leads to* idolatry, or *resembles* idolatry, or *often accompanies* idolatry. He identifies it as idolatry, in the same breath as a vice list with no images, altars, or foreign gods anywhere in view. Covetousness is a wholly interior state — an excessive, disordered desire. Paul's placement of it inside the definition of idolatry is the direct textual warrant for the whole claim this document makes: an inward, disordered affection is not idolatry's cousin. It is idolatry's substance, with or without a physical object.

**The prophetic pattern (spiritual adultery)** — Hosea and Jeremiah repeatedly describe Israel's idolatry using marital-betrayal language (Hosea 2; Jeremiah 3:20; Ezekiel 16), not primarily image-worship language. The offense named is a *turning of the heart's loyalty* away from the covenant partner — betrayal is the operative category, not craftsmanship of an idol. Israel's literal idols are downstream evidence of a prior, more fundamental heart-reorientation; the idols are not themselves the root offense. (This pattern is already load-bearing elsewhere in this model — see [[Covenant Identity — Disordered Affections and the Diagnostic Entry — Mechanism]], "The Biblical Frame," for the Hosea 2 / Jeremiah 2:13 exposition in the mechanism register. This document draws on the same texts to make the *general* claim rather than the mechanism claim.)

## Why Affections Themselves Are Not the Problem

This distinction is the difference between a defensible claim and a claim that will (correctly) draw pushback. Scripture commands strong affection — for God, neighbor, family, justice, beauty, truth (Matt 22:37, 39; Rom 12:9-10; Phil 4:8). The claim is not that feeling deeply, wanting things, or loving people is itself disordered. Affections become idol-like — not simply strong, but *idolatrous* — along three axes:

**Disordered** — loving a good thing *more than* God. The object is legitimate; the ranking is wrong. Loving one's spouse is commanded; loving one's spouse in a way that displaces the love owed to God is the same love, misordered.

**Misdirected** — placing ultimate hope, security, or identity in something created rather than in the Creator. The category error is not the presence of hope but its terminus. Created things were never designed to bear the weight of ultimate hope; when they are asked to, they function as a god even though nobody involved intends worship.

**Controlling** — allowing a desire to govern decisions more than God's revealed will does. The marker here is functional authority: what actually determines the choice under pressure. A desire that consistently outvotes conscience, conviction, or command has assumed a governing role Scripture reserves for God.

None of these are about the *intensity* of the affection. A quiet, low-intensity attachment can be fully idolatrous if it occupies the governing position; a very intense, even overwhelming love (grief, fear, longing) is not automatically idolatrous if it has not displaced God's place in the heart's hierarchy. This is the point most likely to surprise a client and most likely to defuse defensiveness once named clearly: **the diagnostic axis is not how much you feel, but what the feeling has been asked to do.**

## The Diagnostic Question

The most useful single question for surfacing this, adapted from the functional-idolatry tradition already cited in this model (Powlison, Keller — see [[Covenant Identity — God-Exclusion Map]] Source Log):

> "What do I believe I must have in order to be whole, secure, or truly happy?"

If the answer is ultimately anything other than God — a person, an outcome, a role, a possession, an approval rating — that thing may be functioning as an idol of the heart, whether or not the client would ever use religious language to describe their relationship to it. This question is deliberately structured to surface *function* rather than *belief content*, which is why it works even with clients who hold entirely correct theology on paper (see [[Covenant Identity — Functional vs. Confessional Theology — 20 Diagnostic Contrasts]] for the broader pattern this exemplifies).

## The Positive Vision — Not Suppression, Reordering

This is the part of the claim most necessary to state explicitly, because without it the whole framework reads as a demand to love less, which is neither what Scripture teaches nor what the model practices. The biblical call is not to suppress affection but to rightly order it (see also [[Covenant Identity — Set Your Mind on Things Above — Biblical Case]] for the parallel positive-formation argument regarding Col 3:1-2). When love for God holds the position Scripture reserves for it, other loves are not diminished — they are relocated to their proper place and become capable of being enjoyed *as gifts* rather than depended on *as gods*. The distinction that does all the diagnostic and pastoral work is:

- Receiving something as a gift, held with gratitude and open hands, versus
- Depending on that same thing to do for the heart what only God can do.

The same relationship, role, or possession can sit on either side of that line. Coaching work does not ask a client to want less. It asks what the wanting has been asked to secure — and helps relocate that load-bearing weight to the only place designed to carry it.

## Client-Facing Translation

Not client-facing as written above — the apparatus (Exodus 20:3, Colossians 3:5, "idolatry" as a category label) risks landing as accusation rather than invitation if introduced directly, especially early in an engagement. In session, this claim is already operative in gentler, function-first language via the existing tools:

- [[Covenant Identity — God-Exclusion Map]] — opens with a curiosity frame ("where does God actually show up in your day-to-day experience with this?"), not an idolatry frame
- [[Coaching Preparation/Session — Active/Client Tools/09 — Functional Savior Audit]] — the client-facing worksheet already built around this exact claim, using "functional savior" rather than "idol" as the working term
- The diagnostic question above ("what do I believe I must have...") is usable directly with clients; it does the same work as the doctrinal claim without requiring the client to accept the theological label first

**When to name the word "idolatry" directly with a client:** Only after the functional pattern has already been named and confirmed by the client in their own language, and only when the client has enough theological literacy and trust in the relationship for the term to land as clarifying rather than condemning. Naming the mechanism (Colossians 3:5, the disordered/misdirected/controlling axes) is for the coach's own defensibility — with peers, supervisors, or a client who directly asks "why are you calling this idolatry?" — not the default opening move in session.

## Anticipated Objection

**"This just relabels ordinary human love as sin."** It does not, if the disordered/misdirected/controlling distinction above is held clearly. The claim is not that loving a person, a role, or an achievement is sinful. It is that any created thing — however good — becomes idol-like the moment it is asked to occupy the position Scripture reserves for God: ultimate security, ultimate worth, ultimate hope, governing authority over decisions. The love is not the offense. The misplacement is. This is also why the model's diagnostic language (see [[Covenant Identity — God-Exclusion Map]]) deliberately avoids leading with an idolatry frame in session — the pastoral risk of the label landing as an accusation of sin rather than a diagnosis of misplaced weight is real, and this document's own framing above (gift vs. dependence) is the direct answer to it.
