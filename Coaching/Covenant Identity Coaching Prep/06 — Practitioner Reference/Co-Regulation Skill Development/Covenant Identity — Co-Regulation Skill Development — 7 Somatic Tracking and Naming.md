# Covenant Identity — Co-Regulation Skill Development — 7 Somatic Tracking and Naming

*Part 7 of 7. See [[Covenant Identity — Co-Regulation Skill Development — Index]] for the full set. Depends on [[Covenant Identity — Co-Regulation Skill Development — 1 Self-Regulation Foundation]] (accurate observation requires a settled observer) and [[Covenant Identity — Co-Regulation Skill Development — 5 Attuned Responsiveness]] (timing judgment).*

## Target Capacity

Two distinct skills combined: (1) accurately observing a client's somatic cues — breath, color, posture, muscle tone, voice — in real time, and (2) naming them in language that invites curiosity rather than triggering self-consciousness or shame. Ogden's Sensorimotor Psychotherapy approach uses tentative, non-diagnostic phrasing ("I'm noticing... what do you notice?") rather than declarative statements ("You're anxious") — the adapted skill here, not the full clinical protocol.

## Why This Matters in CIC

[[Covenant Identity — Practitioner Competency Framework]] Domain 3 already directs coaches to "read client body signals without projecting: observe first, ask second." [[Exceptional Coaching Practice — What Is Missed, Meaningful & Important]] Section IX gives a full question bank for this ("I notice your jaw tightened when you said that. What is that?"). This document trains the observation and timing skill underneath that question bank — CIC explicitly names the body as carrying schema and false-identity material the client's verbal processing may be actively managing, which makes this one of the more diagnostically load-bearing skills in the whole set, not just a rapport tool.

## Mastery Requirements

- A working vocabulary of specific, nameable somatic markers (not just "tense" or "upset")
- Restraint: knowing when *not* to name something and to simply hold space instead
- Timing — naming too early, mid-experience, can pull a client out of a felt state into cognitive analysis; the skill is waiting for a natural pause

## Training Protocol

**Phase 1 — Vocabulary building (solo).**
Build a personal reference list of specific somatic markers across categories: breath (shallow/held/rapid/sighing), posture (collapsed/braced/leaning away/leaning in), color (flushed/pale), voice (thinning/dropping/speeding), hands (clenched/still/fidgeting), eyes (welling/darting/fixed). Most people default to two or three vague terms under pressure; this phase exists to widen what you actually have available to notice and name.

**Phase 2 — Observation-only practice (solo, using any conversation).**
In ordinary conversations this week, practice silently noting somatic shifts in the other person without saying anything out loud — just building the observation muscle. Compare what you noticed against your Phase 1 vocabulary list; are you actually using the range, or defaulting to the same one or two markers?

**Phase 3 — Tentative-language drill (solo, scripted).**
Take five somatic observations from Phase 2 (or invented ones) and practice phrasing each two ways: a declarative version ("You're getting defensive") and a tentative, curiosity-inviting version ("I noticed your voice just changed — what's that about?"). Say both out loud. Notice how differently they land even to your own ear.

**Phase 4 — Timing and restraint practice (requires a partner or live session).**
This is the piece that can't be fully drilled solo: practicing *when* to name something versus letting it pass, and reading whether naming pulled the person toward the experience or away from it into analysis. Requires real-time feedback from another person, ideally a supervisor who can flag when a naming interrupted rather than deepened.

## Solo-Safe vs. Requires Supervision

Phases 1–3 are solo-safe and buildable now. Phase 4's timing judgment is genuinely a live-feedback skill; hold structured drilling of it for the coaching-craft supervisor relationship per [[Covenant Identity — Pre-Practice Readiness Action Plan]], the same guardrail already applied elsewhere in this set.

## Self-Assessment Markers

- Your Phase 1 vocabulary list has grown past the two or three defaults you started with, and you're actually drawing on the fuller list in Phase 2 observation
- In Phase 3, the tentative-language version is now your first instinct, not something you have to consciously reach for
- Once Phase 4 begins: a supervisor or partner confirms your namings more often deepen than interrupt the client's experience

## Common Failure Modes

- **Naming everything you notice** — over-naming pulls a client out of felt experience into cognitive analysis exactly when the felt experience is the more valuable material; restraint is as much the skill as accuracy
- **Declarative rather than tentative phrasing** — "you're anxious" invites defense or correction; "I'm noticing something in your breathing — what's happening?" invites the client's own report, which is more diagnostically reliable than your inference
- **Projecting instead of observing** — naming what you assume is happening rather than what you actually saw; this is the specific error Domain 3's "observe first, ask second" line is written to prevent
