# Covenant Identity — Image-Protective Patterns (Narcissistic Patterns) — Practitioner Reference

*Practitioner reference. Internal use only. Governs how CIC writes and speaks about narcissistic patterns.*

*Status: seed document. Part A (guardrails) built 2026-10-13. Parts B–D wait on Andrew's video-sourced source notes. See Build Plan.*

**Related:** [[Covenant Identity — Psychological Constructs — Reverse Connection Reference]] · [[Covenant Identity — Character Wound Diagnostic Tool]] · [[Covenant Identity — Crisis & Referral Protocol]] · [[Covenant Identity — Self-Supervision Template]] · [[Covenant Identity — Terminology & Language Reference]]

## What This Document Is For

The word *narcissistic* reaches a coach in three ways.

- A client uses it about someone else ("my husband is a narcissist").
- A client recognizes the pattern in themself.
- The coach recognizes it in themself.

Society also uses the word far outside clinical settings. It is applied loosely, often to anyone who seems self-absorbed or hurtful, and not always accurately. Practitioners are confused by it too. The DSM-5 lists nine criteria, and confusion remains about how the behaviors actually present.

This document sets four rules that keep CIC's use of the word accurate and safe.

1. CIC language stays behavioral and non-clinical. The clinical term follows in parentheses for association.
2. No client is labeled. No absent third party is diagnosed.
3. Every claim imported from psychology carries a tag showing how well it is supported.
4. The line between what coaching can hold and what needs clinical care is stated once, here.

**What the coach does differently because this exists:** Before writing or saying anything about narcissistic patterns (in a session, a document, or a client-facing piece), the coach checks it against Part A. A claim with no evidence tag does not go in.

**Scope:** CIC only. A version for Relational Performance Advisory or Formation & Transition would be a separate document, built for that track's evidence base and audience.

## Part A — Guardrails

### Guardrail 1 — Language Convention: Plain Wording First, Clinical Term in Parentheses

**Rule.** Write the non-clinical description first. Put the clinical term in parentheses after it.

**Why.** Clinical terms carry diagnostic weight that CIC does not hold. The parenthetical lets a reader connect CIC's wording to what a client read online, or to what a clinician would say. CIC does not claim the clinical term as its own.

**Note.** The plain phrases below are descriptive. They are *not* new CIC terms of art. CIC's formal diagnostic terms remain False Covenant Object and False Identity Structure ([[Covenant Identity — False Covenant Objects & False Identity Structures — Terms Reference]]).

| Plain wording                                                            | Clinical association                                                   | Note                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| image-protective pattern                                                 | (narcissistic pattern; narcissistic defenses)                          | The umbrella phrase in this document.                                                                                   |
| superiority-displaying form                                              | (grandiose or overt narcissism)                                        | Visible self-importance, entitlement, a need to be seen as above others.                                                |
| shame-heavy form                                                         | (vulnerable or covert narcissism)                                      | Hypersensitivity to criticism, withdrawal, envy, quiet self-focus. Can look anxious, humble, or self-critical.[^pincus] |
| needing admiration in order to feel worth                                | (narcissistic supply; admiration-seeking)                              | Ongoing Co-dependency of the admiration from others to sense personal value                                             |
| wounded-pride reaction                                                   | (narcissistic injury)                                                  | When it escalates to rage or cold withdrawal: (narcissistic rage).                                                      |
| treating people as an audience, a mirror, or a resource                  | (interpersonal exploitativeness; using others to regulate self-esteem) |                                                                                                                         |
| difficulty registering or caring about another person's inner experience | (impaired empathy)                                                     | [P] Popular accounts say "no empathy." The research picture may be more mixed. Detail pending Part B.                   |
| an expectation of special treatment                                      | (entitlement)                                                          |                                                                                                                         |
| a self-appraisal that swings between inflated and deflated               | (unstable self-esteem regulation)                                      |                                                                                                                         |
| a pattern at personality-disorder level                                  | (Narcissistic Personality Disorder, NPD)                               | Only a licensed clinician determines this.                                                                              |

**Using the common word.** CIC may use *narcissistic* as a bridge word in two cases only. The first is when a client uses it. The second is when a coach needs to say what the pattern is called outside CIC. In both cases, describe the behavior beside the word.

In CIC's own voice, use the adjective for a pattern ("a narcissistic pattern"). Do not use the noun for a person ("a narcissist"). [D] CIC treats a false identity structure as something built around a person. The noun fixes the person inside the category.

**When a client labels someone else.** A client says, "My husband is a narcissist." The coach receives the experience. The coach neither confirms nor denies the label, because the coach has heard one side of one relationship. The coach asks what the person does, what happens next, and what the client does in response. [D] Full guidance is pending (Build Plan, Part D). If the client describes abuse, coercion, or fear for their safety, go to the [[Covenant Identity — Crisis & Referral Protocol]].

### Guardrail 2 — Three Registers of the Word

The same word names three different things. Mixing them is the main source of confusion.

| Register | What it names | Who may determine it | CIC position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Everyday word** | A loose label for people who seem self-absorbed, arrogant, or hurtful. Often used of absent third parties. Accuracy varies widely. | Anyone. There is no standard. | Listen for the behaviors under the word. Do not adopt the label. Do not argue with it. |
| **Trait-level pattern** | A dimension of personality that everyone carries to some degree. Research describes two main expressions, superiority-displaying (grandiose) and shame-heavy (vulnerable). One person can show both at different times.[^pincus] | Research instruments. In coaching, only the coach's working hypothesis. | **Coach with care.** Hold as a hypothesis. Confirm it against session observation. Matches the scope flag ◐ in the [[Covenant Identity — Psychological Constructs — Reverse Connection Reference]]. |
| **Clinical disorder** | Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD). A long-standing pattern present across contexts, with significant impairment or distress. DSM-5 requires at least five of nine criteria.[^dsm] | A licensed clinician only. | **Refer or co-care.** Matches the scope flag ○ in the Reverse Connection Reference. |

**Existing CIC positions.** Three documents already place this boundary. The [[Covenant Identity — Psychological Constructs — Reverse Connection Reference]] flags the pattern "coaching with care" and NPD as "refer." The [[Covenant Identity — Character Wound Diagnostic Tool]] says clinical presentations resembling the Warrior and False Noble types, including narcissistic personality disorder, "require clinical assessment, not coaching deepening." The [[Covenant Identity Coaching — White Paper]] and [[Covenant Identity Coaching — Full Model Reference]] each list "personality disorder patterns" among the presentations coaching refers out.

**A gap, now closed.** Until 2026-10-13 the [[Covenant Identity — Crisis & Referral Protocol]] did not mention personality-disorder-level presentations. It was searched for "personality," "Cluster B," and "narcissis," with no match. Andrew approved a fix the same day. Section 2 of the protocol now has a personality-disorder-level bullet that points here.

**Draft referral indicators.** [D] These are a working draft. They are not yet in the Crisis & Referral Protocol. Review them with the scope-boundary supervisor (priority 1 in [[Covenant Identity — Pre-Practice Readiness Action Plan]]). Any one of them moves the question from "coach with care" toward "refer or co-care."

- The pattern shows up across most areas of the client's life, not one sphere.
- The client's behavior is causing ongoing serious harm to others, such as coercive control, abuse, or sustained cruelty.
- A threat to the client's image produces rage, collapse, or withdrawal that the alliance cannot settle within the session or between sessions.
- The client has suicidal thoughts, or acts on them, after a humiliation or failure. Suicide risk after perceived humiliation is documented in the clinical literature for people with these patterns.[^ronningstam] The Crisis & Referral Protocol governs.
- The client cannot tolerate any look at their own felt inadequacy without attacking, devaluing, or leaving the coach. Slowing the pace does not change it.
- The client uses sessions mainly to collect admiration, or to recruit the coach against other people.

### The DSM-5 Reference List (for association, not for tallying)

These are the nine DSM-5 criteria for NPD, paraphrased. Plain wording leads. The clinical name follows in parentheses.[^dsm]

| # | Plain wording | Clinical association |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sees themself as more important, talented, or accomplished than the facts support | (grandiose sense of self-importance) |
| 2 | Preoccupied with fantasies of unlimited success, power, brilliance, beauty, or ideal love | (grandiose fantasy) |
| 3 | Believes they are special and unique, and that only special or high-status people can understand them or be fit company | (belief in being special) |
| 4 | Needs excessive admiration | (requires admiration) |
| 5 | Expects especially favorable treatment or automatic compliance | (entitlement) |
| 6 | Takes advantage of others to reach their own ends | (interpersonal exploitation) |
| 7 | Unwilling or unable to recognize or identify with the feelings and needs of others | (lack of empathy) |
| 8 | Often envious of others, or believes others are envious of them | (envy) |
| 9 | Shows arrogant, haughty behavior or attitudes | (arrogance) |

Three cautions govern how this list is used.

1. **It is not a coach's checklist.** Counting matches is not diagnosing. A diagnosis needs a pervasive pattern across contexts and impairment, judged by a clinician. [E]
2. **It describes mostly the superiority-displaying form.** The shame-heavy form (hypersensitivity, withdrawal, shame, a devalued sense of self) is poorly captured by these nine items. That gap is a main source of confusion among practitioners and the public. [E][^pincus] DSM-5 also offers a research-stage alternative model (Section III). It defines NPD through impairments in self and interpersonal functioning plus the traits of grandiosity and attention seeking.[^dsm] Reviews report that those two traits capture the superiority-displaying form better than the shame-heavy one. [E, specific review not pinned in this pass]
3. **Context changes meaning.** Several items resemble ordinary behavior in some roles and life stages, such as a confident executive or an adolescent. Pervasiveness and impairment separate them. [D]

### Guardrail 3 — Every Claim Carries an Evidence Tag

No narcissism-related claim enters a CIC document untagged. The tag goes at the claim. The source goes in a footnote at the end of the note.

| Tag | Meaning | Allowed use |
|---|---|---|
| **[E]** | Research-established. Published in a primary source such as a peer-reviewed study, the DSM, or a validated instrument. | May ground a scope or referral decision. |
| **[T]** | Clinical theory. Influential and built largely from clinical observation (for example Kohut, Kernberg, Winnicott). | Informs hypotheses. Does not ground a referral alone. |
| **[P]** | Practitioner observation. A video or popular-clinician claim not yet traced to a primary source. | Recognition aid only. **Never** grounds a scope or referral decision. |
| **[D]** | CIC design judgment. An inference made for this model. | Valid as CIC's own choice. Must be labeled. Review with the scope-boundary supervisor when it touches referral. |

A [P] claim can move up to [E] or [T] when someone finds its primary source. Until then it stays [P], even when it is widely repeated.

### Guardrail 4 — The Developmental Account: Established, Theorized, Disputed

Where the pattern comes from is the least settled part of this topic. The table separates what is measured from what is theorized.

| Claim | Tag | Source and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Differences in narcissistic traits can be measured in children from about age 7 | [E] | Brummelman et al. (2015): 565 children aged 7–12, four waves six months apart.[^brummelman] |
| Parental overvaluation (treating the child as more special than others) predicted rising narcissism. Parental warmth predicted self-esteem, not narcissism. | [E] | Same study. One prospective community sample. Overvaluation was parent-reported. Genetics, temperament, and reverse causation (a needy child eliciting more praise) are only partly ruled out.[^brummelman] |
| Lack of parental warmth did **not** predict narcissism in that study | [E] | Same limits. The study measured subclinical self-inflation (the superiority-displaying side) in a community sample of children. It cannot speak to the shame-heavy form or to pathological narcissism.[^kealy] Adult retrospective studies point the other way for the shame-heavy form: childhood adversity predicted it, and parental overvaluation did not (small effects).[^nguyen] |
| A shame-and-wound route: early shame, invalidation, or conditional regard leaves a fragile self protected by an inflated image | [T] | Kohut, Kernberg, Winnicott.[^classic] Strong clinical-case support. Weaker prospective support. This is the route CIC's wound-based diagnostics were built around. |
| Normal early grandiosity and a need for mirroring in toddler and early-childhood years, with later separation phases | [T] | Mahler, Kohut.[^classic] The age bands (rapprochement about 15–24 months; grandiose self about ages 2–4) come from psychoanalytic theory. They were reconstructed from clinical and infant observation. **They are not validated predictors. Do not cite them as research findings.** |
| On average, narcissism is higher in youth and declines through adulthood. In one longitudinal study of 237 adults followed from 18 to 41, narcissism declined on average and about 3% increased. | [E] | Wetzel et al. (2020).[^wetzel] Roberts et al. (2010) is supporting commentary and carries an age-versus-generation confound.[^roberts] |
| Childhood narcissistic traits are continuous with adult NPD | Not established | No source reviewed in this pass shows it. Group-level trait stability is supported. Predicting any one child's adult outcome is not. |

**What follows for CIC.** [D] There may be two routes into the structure: being overvalued, and being wounded. The second route is what the Character Wound Diagnostic Tool was built for. The first is rewarded conditioning, not injury.

**Routing rule (decided by Andrew, 2026-10-13).** [D]

1. Run the injury test in the Character Wound Diagnostic Tool's "Before Typing" section. Is there a person, relationship, or event the pattern protects against? If not, use the Pattern Origin arc in Phase 2.
2. Overvaluation alone does not pass the injury test. Being told one is special is rewarded conditioning. Start on Pattern Origin. Hold False Noble as a hypothesis.
3. Two further checks decide close cases. First, which way the core belief runs: "I am not enough" fits the False Noble. "I am more than others" or "I deserve more" does not. Second, whether love was felt as at risk: the False Noble wound is love that was conditional or inconsistent. Both checks are now in "Before Typing."
4. Mixed case (a superiority belief with some conditional-love history): Pattern Origin leads. Watch for a person-shaped injury or an "I was afraid to lose their love" story, and switch to wound typing if one surfaces. The Pattern Origin question set already contains the redirect: "Is there a person, or is it more like an atmosphere?"
5. Behavior under threat cannot decide alone. A client with learned superiority who answers threat with anger can look like a Warrior without a betrayal wound. A client who performs harder under pressure can look like a False Noble without an ambivalence wound. Only the injury test separates them.
6. Log the routing call in the [[Covenant Identity — Diagnostic Calibration Log]] and hold it lightly until signals converge across sessions. The log's scope was extended to routing calls on 2026-10-13. It records the four routing signals and closes each entry out against later evidence.
7. Not adopted: widening "ambivalence" to include being loved as an extension of the parent's image. No evidence supports it, and it would blur a clean type. Reconsider if live clients show the pattern.

### Guardrail 4b — Is Shame or a Wound the Baseline of Every Pattern?

Andrew's working belief was that image-protective patterns are wound-based. A research pass on 2026-10-13 tested that belief against the literature.

**Finding.** Not as a universal. Shame and wound are well supported as the baseline of the shame-heavy form. They are not supported as the baseline of the superiority-displaying form. [E, correlational. Limits below.]

| Form | Shame | Attachment | Maltreatment or neglect | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shame-heavy** (vulnerable or covert narcissism) | Moderate-to-strong positive link. Reviewers treat shame as its core organizing affect.[^kesman] | Preoccupied strongest, fearful moderate, dismissive weak, secure negative.[^mohay] | Small positive link (about r = .20). Neglect is stronger than physical abuse.[^gao] | Wound-based is well supported. |
| **Superiority-displaying** (grandiose or overt narcissism) | Small negative link on the standard scale. More variable on the Pathological Narcissism Inventory.[^kesman] | No significant link to insecure styles.[^mohay] | Tiny link (about r = .09), larger in samples under 18 (about .19).[^gao] Recalled parental overvaluation shows a small link in adults.[^nguyen] | Not wound-based by these data. |
| **Clinical NPD** | Low shame proneness, high shame aversion, elevated implicit state shame.[^kesman] | Not covered here. | Not covered here. | Mixed. Latent shame is plausible and unproven. One clinical sample showed lower explicit self-esteem than controls and no implicit difference.[^vater] |

Five further findings bear on the baseline question.

- **No hidden low self-esteem under the superiority form.** Meta-analyses found no consistent mask of low implicit self-esteem behind narcissism.[^bosson] [E]
- **Fragile does not mean low.** The self-view is positive but contingent. It rises and falls with feedback.[^morf] After an insult, aggression went toward its source.[^bushman] [E]
- **One person moves between states.** Across 862 people and 36,631 momentary reports, people high in the superiority form also showed vulnerable states. People high in the shame-heavy form stayed mostly vulnerable. The two states rarely occurred in the same moment.[^edershile] [E]
- **The shared core is not shame.** One model places entitled self-importance at the core of both forms.[^krizan] Another describes two routes to a grandiose self: self-promotion, and antagonistic defense against failure.[^back] [E for the models. The approach-versus-avoidance wording comes from a secondary source.]
- **Genes carry a large share.** A 2014 twin study put grandiosity at 23% heritable and entitlement at 35%.[^luo] [E] A 2026 twin-family report claims about 50% genetic, the rest non-shared experience, and no detectable shared-family effect.[^backtwin] [Press-level only.] If the second holds, parenting explanations are weaker than the retrospective studies suggest. Non-shared experiences (peers, individual treatment, trauma) could still matter.

**What could not be settled.**

- Hidden shame under the superiority form is neither shown nor ruled out. People may under-report shame. Implicit measures are noisy.
- Nearly all of the evidence is correlational, retrospective, or self-report. The wound effects are small.
- Several sources were read at abstract level or through press coverage. The Sources section says which.

**CIC stance.** [D] Wound-based is the default hypothesis for shame-heavy and mixed presentations. Superiority-displaying presentations get an explicit alternative: learned superiority or temperament. Four signals discriminate: the injury test, the direction of the core belief, whether love was felt as at risk, and the response under threat. Shame or withdrawal under threat points to the wound branch. Anger or contempt points to the alternative, or to anger covering shame. No single signal is proof.

### Guardrail 5 — Design Premises Recorded From Andrew (2026-10-13)

These two premises shape how CIC approaches this population. Both are [D]. Each is stated with its support and its limits.

**Premise 1 — Many people shaped by early shame carry image-protective patterns in a moderated form, and cause harm mainly when their image is threatened.**

- *Support.* Research treats narcissism as a dimension, not an on/off category.[^pincus] In a laboratory study, insulted participants high in narcissism aggressed most against the person who insulted them. Aggression toward an innocent third party stayed low.[^bushman] [E, with limits: a laboratory noise-blast task with a superiority-measuring scale. It is not a study of abuse or of the shame-heavy form.]
- *Limit.* "Harms only when provoked" is **not** a safe screening assumption. What counts as provocation is often ordinary: a disagreement, a correction, being overlooked. The person who receives the reaction is usually the nearest family member or colleague. The coach asks what threatens the image, what happens next, and who is nearby when it does.

**Premise 2 — High-functioning Christians who carry these patterns are suffering, and they are a high-fit coaching population.**

- *Support.* Research describes the shame-heavy form as marked by a devalued sense of self, helplessness, emptiness, and shame.[^pincus] [E] It tends to co-occur with anxiety and depression. [E, later work, specific source not pinned in this pass] Help-seeking in people with these patterns is widely reported to be driven by that distress, not by the pattern itself. [T, source not pinned in this pass] CIC's diagnostic chain (disordered affection, false covenant object, false identity structure) describes the pattern's logic closely. The existing False Noble mapping already points there. [D]
- *Limit 1.* "Ideal" is not established. CIC has no live client hours yet ([[Covenant Identity — Methodology & Readiness Review for Clinical Consultation]]). Call this population **high-fit and high-difficulty** until live evidence says otherwise.
- *Limit 2.* The same defenses that make the person high-functioning work against two of CIC's own steps. Those defenses are image management, control, intellectualizing, and resistance to looking inadequate. The steps are felt inadequacy (Node 3) and receptive posture (Node 4) in [[Covenant Identity Coaching — Theory of Change]]. [D]
- *Limit 3.* Early idealization of the coach, followed by sharp disappointment, is a known alliance risk with these patterns. [T] Plan for the rupture before it happens.
- *Limit 4.* Covenant identity language ("chosen," "beloved," "seated with Christ") can be absorbed as a new source of superiority instead of received as grace. [D] A test the coach can apply: does the truth lower the client's need for admiration (more freedom to be ordinary, to be corrected), or does it raise the client's investment in being seen as special?
- *Limit 5.* People who seek coaching are people who recognize a problem. Do not generalize from this group to everyone who carries the pattern. [D]

### Guardrail 6 — The Coach's Own Pattern

A coach who carries an image-protective pattern, from lived history or from the present, holds an asset and a risk.

- **Asset.** Recognition from the inside. The coach knows what the pattern protects.
- **Risks.** Enjoying a client's idealization. Becoming defensive when corrected. Over-identifying with the client. Under-seeing a pattern because it feels familiar. [D]

The control is the [[Covenant Identity — Self-Supervision Template]] (Wound Pattern Intersection and Cross-Session Pattern Watch) plus the scope-boundary supervisor. A narcissistic-pattern watch was added to Section 5 of the Self-Supervision Template on 2026-10-13. This document records no personal history on purpose.

Direction and "small" size verified through an abstract summary. Results taken from a secondary summary. The PDF could not be read in-session. Confirm before citing.
