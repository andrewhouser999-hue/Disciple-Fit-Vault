# Scripture-Based Bible Lessons for Sessions

## Purpose of This Document

This is a running pool of short, scripture-based lessons Andrew can draw from during or between CIC sessions. Each lesson supports a biblical perspective the client may need at a given moment. Andrew adds lessons as he collects them.

A lesson is **not** a tool and **not** a cause of change. It is material the Spirit may use once the coaching relationship has created the conditions for it. Use a lesson when a client's own words or state make it fit. Do not run a lesson because it is next in the list.

**Posture rule for every lesson:** offered as an invitation to see, never as a correction to apply. If a lesson would land as "you are doing this wrong," hold it back.

## Lesson 1 — God's Second Question: Scripts, Noise, and the Low Whisper

**Passage:** 1 Kings 19:1–18 (ESV)

### The one-line lesson

A story we keep repeating can sound true and still leave out what God knows. God asked Elijah the same question twice, and the second time was after the noise stopped.

### What the text says

- Elijah fled from Jezebel's threat (19:2–3), traveled to Beersheba, then about forty days to **Horeb**, the mount of God (19:8). *(Not Mount Hermon. Horeb is Sinai.)*
- Before God asked anything, an angel let him sleep and fed him twice (19:5–8). He had asked to die (19:4).
- In the cave, the word of the LORD asks: **"What are you doing here, Elijah?"** (19:9)
- Elijah answers (19:10): *"I have been very jealous for the LORD, the God of hosts. For the people of Israel have forsaken your covenant, thrown down your altars, and killed your prophets with the sword, and I, even I only, am left, and they seek my life, to take it away."*
- God tells him to stand on the mount. A great wind, an earthquake, and a fire pass. *"The LORD was not in"* any of them. After the fire, *"the sound of a low whisper"* (19:11–12). The KJV renders this "still small voice."
- Elijah hears it, wraps his face in his cloak, and stands at the cave entrance (19:13).
- A voice asks the **same question**: "What are you doing here, Elijah?" (19:13)
- Elijah gives the **same answer**, in the same words (19:14).
- God does not argue with the answer. He gives Elijah a task (go back, anoint Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha) and a correcting fact: *"I will leave seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to Baal"* (19:15–18).

### The lawyer's input

*(Andrew's note; source to be added: the lawyer, name or context, and whether this is a stated courtroom practice or a personal rule of thumb.)*

A lawyer cross-examining a witness may ask the same question twice. A person answering from what happened tends to reword the answer, because they are recalling an event. A person answering from a rehearsed account tends to give it word for word, because they are repeating a script. The repeated, identical answer exposes the script.

**Treat this as a pattern worth noticing, not as proof.** People also repeat well-worn true accounts nearly verbatim, especially painful ones. The technique raises a question. It does not deliver a verdict.

### The lesson

**1. God asked twice, and the answer did not change.** In Hebrew, Elijah's two answers (19:10 and 19:14) are word for word the same. That is exactly what the lawyer's test would flag. Elijah had settled on an account, and the wind, earthquake, and fire did not move it.

**2. The script is not a lie about God. It is a story about God's absence.** Elijah's account includes God ("jealous for the LORD"). What it leaves out is God's agency, God's care, and the facts that contradict it. "I, even I only, am left" is not true: Obadiah hid a hundred prophets (18:4), and God names seven thousand (19:18). Do not call it fabrication. It is a true core (the covenant was broken, Jezebel did threaten him) wrapped in overstatement, with the Lord written out of the next move. *(This is the pattern the God-Exclusion Map names.)*

**3. God is not in the noise.** Wind, earthquake, and fire are the loud ways God was known at Sinai (Exodus 19:16–19). Here God shows Elijah something different: the Lord is not found in the loudest thing. Elijah had just come from a fire-from-heaven victory on Carmel (ch. 18). He had learned to expect God in the dramatic. The low whisper corrects that expectation.

**4. The second question comes after the noise, and it is gentler than the first.** The first question was asked into Elijah's exhaustion and fear. The second was asked after he had stood in silence. The text gives us the order. It does not say the silence changed his answer. It did not. What follows the second answer is **God's own word**: a commission and a correction. The movement forward comes from what God says, not from Elijah's insight.

**5. God is not gathering evidence.** A lawyer asks to expose. God already knows the answer. His question is an invitation: "What are you doing here?" is also "Why are you here, and not where I sent you?" The repeated question gives Elijah a second chance to answer differently. It is kindness, not cross-examination.

### Use when

- A client explains a situation in nearly the same words each time it comes up, and the explanation ends in "so there's nothing I can do" or "it's all on me."
- A client's week is loud (schedule, media, communication) and they say they "can't tell" what is God and what is fear, drive, or pressure.
- A client's account is mostly true but leaves out God's agency, presence, or care (see God-Exclusion Map).
- A client is depleted and cannot yet receive more truth.

### Do not use when

- The client is physically or emotionally depleted and has not been cared for first. Elijah was fed and slept before God asked anything. Care comes before the question.
- Using it would turn the coach into a cross-examiner. Never ask the same question twice in a row as a trap. Ask the second time after a pause, a breath, or a shift in the room, and as a real invitation.
- The client treats any quiet inner impression as God's voice. This lesson does **not** say that. In the passage the whisper arrives with content that fits what God has already said in His covenant. Impressions are weighed against Scripture.
- Signs of depression, suicidal thinking, or trauma are present. Elijah's text includes a request to die (19:4). That is a referral question, not a lesson; follow the Crisis & Referral Protocol.

### In-session move

After the client gives an account, wait. Then, once the room has slowed, ask once more, gently: **"Say that again, but slower. What is happening here?"** Notice whether the answer is reworded or word for word. If word for word, do not name it a script. Ask: **"Where is God in that story?"** and let silence do the work.

*(Formation posture: the Spirit is the one who exposes. The coach creates the quiet.)*

### Between-session practice

Three minutes of silence, once a day this week. No agenda and no asking for answers. One line to write afterward: **"What was louder than I expected?"** The aim is to notice the noise, not to produce a word from God.

### Limits of the text

- The text does not say Elijah rehearsed his answer over and over on the way. That is an inference from the identical wording. Present it as a likely reading, not a fact.
- The text does not say the whisper is easy to recognize. It says Elijah recognized the Lord's presence enough to cover his face.
- The text does not say God never speaks in loud ways. He did at Sinai, and Job 38 is a whirlwind. The point is that God's presence is not **identified by** loudness.
- "Low whisper" (Hebrew *qol demamah daqqah*) has a range of senses. Some translations render it "a sound of sheer silence" (NRSV) or "a gentle whisper" (NIV). Whether a voice or a hush is meant is contested. The lesson works either way: the contrast with the noise carries it.
- Elijah's second answer being unchanged is part of the text. The lesson should not claim the whisper replaced his beliefs on the spot. Change in the story is shown to come through God's commission and correction afterward.
