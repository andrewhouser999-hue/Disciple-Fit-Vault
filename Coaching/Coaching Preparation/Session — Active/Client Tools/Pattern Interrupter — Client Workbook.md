# Pattern Interrupter — Client Workbook

---

## Before You Begin: Read This First

This workbook is for a specific kind of stuck place: not a belief you hold, and not a self-concept problem — a **pattern you run on another person**, automatically, that contradicts what you actually want for that relationship. It usually shows up as: "I know better. I've even decided better. And I still do the same thing when the moment comes."

That gap is the target. A pattern like this was almost always *learned* — watched and absorbed from someone who modeled it for you — before it was ever chosen. It lives in the body as a rehearsed sequence, not in the mind as a belief, which is why deciding to stop hasn't been enough on its own. This workbook works at that level: naming the sequence precisely, naming what it's really trusting instead of God, counting its real cost, and rehearsing a specific, concrete replacement until it becomes what fires instead.

**A boundary worth naming up front:** this tool is for patterns rooted in what was modeled to you and reinforced by habit — not for trauma-level material. If naming the old pattern brings up more than discomfort — flashbacks, a sense of danger, material that feels bigger than "a pattern I picked up" — that's worth naming to your coach directly. It may call for support alongside this work, not instead of it.

**Where this fits alongside other tools:** this workbook is self-sufficient — you don't need to have worked another tool first. But it shares ground with three others in this folder, and it's worth knowing the overlap rather than duplicating effort blind: [[04 — Decisional Balance]] does a fuller version of the Cost work (section 7 here) if you want to go deeper on ambivalence; [[05 — Competing Commitment Map]] does a fuller version of what Anchor Identity (section 8) is drawing on; [[09 — Functional Savior Audit]] does a fuller version of section 6 if one field isn't enough to surface what you're really trusting instead of God. Use this workbook on its own for a specific, named pattern; reach for those if a single field doesn't go deep enough.

**Clinical and theological basis, briefly:** this workbook's structure is built primarily on Habit Reversal Training — developed for physical habits, adapted here to a relational pattern rather than validated for that use directly — with supporting structure from behavioral chain analysis, relapse-prevention research, and a biblical-counseling diagnostic layer (naming what you're functionally trusting instead of God). It is not a new or unproven idea grafted onto Scripture — it adapts a well-documented behavioral approach and pairs it with the same functional-idolatry diagnostic this system already uses elsewhere. (Your coach can point you to the full basis if you're interested.)

## How This Workbook Is Structured

Each pattern you work through has fourteen sections. Here is what each one does and how to complete it. **The order matters:** sections 1–7 diagnose the pattern; section 8 anchors your identity; section 9 — the new pattern — is written *from* that identity, which is why it comes after, not before. If you find yourself wanting to skip to "what should I do instead" before section 8, that pull is worth noticing, not following.

---

### 1. This Activates When
**What it is:** The specific situation that sets the old pattern off.
**What to do:** Name it precisely — not "when I'm stressed" but the actual moment: *who*, *doing what*, in *what setting*. Precision here is what lets you catch it next time, instead of only recognizing it afterward.

---

### 2. Whose Pattern This Is
**What it is:** The person who modeled this for you — a parent, another authority figure, a past relationship.
**What to do:** Name them. Not to blame — to place the pattern outside your character and back into its actual origin. A pattern you learned by watching someone else is not the same thing as who you are; naming its source is often what makes it possible to change at all. *"The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father"* (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV) — a pattern handed to you does not have to be handed on. Breaking it is not disloyalty to where it came from.

---

### 3. Wound or Gap?
**What it is:** A quick diagnostic distinction that shapes how the rest of the work goes.
**What to do:** Ask which is more true — **a wound** (this pattern protected you from something that was done to you, and still carries that charge), or **a gap** (you were simply never shown another way, so this is the only template you have). Often it's some of both. Naming which one is doing more of the work tells you whether you need to grieve something first, or whether you mainly need practice at something new.

---

### 4. The Old Pattern — Step by Step
**What it is:** The actual sequence — not a single description, but the order of moves.
**What to do:** Write it as steps: what happens in your body first, what you do or say first, then next, then next. A vague description ("I get harsh") can't be caught in the moment. A specific sequence can.

---

### 5. What This Pattern Gives Me
**What it is:** The honest payoff — why the pattern is still running even though you don't want it to be.
**What to do:** Be specific and unflattering. Common answers: a sense of control, avoided vulnerability, proof you're not what you fear being seen as. Naming this isn't self-indictment — it's identifying what the replacement pattern will also have to satisfy, or it won't hold.

---

### 6. What Am I Trusting Instead? (Functional Savior Diagnostic)
**What it is:** The theological question underneath section 5's psychological one. The payoff you just named is real, but it's usually serving something deeper — a functional savior, something you're trusting or relying on in place of God, even if you'd never call it that.
**What to do:** Ask directly: when I run this pattern, what am I functionally trusting instead of trusting God with the outcome? Common answers: my own control, my image in front of others, being seen as competent or strong, avoiding a specific fear. Name the idol, not just the payoff — this is a repentance-and-faith target, not only a habit target, and naming it as such is what keeps this from becoming pure behavior modification.
**Why this matters:** A pattern can be behaviorally interrupted without ever being repented of — you can learn a new habit while still trusting the same false savior underneath it. This field exists so the change actually reaches the belief driving the behavior, not just the behavior itself.

---

### 7. What This Pattern Costs — In Two Parts, In This Order

**Why the order matters:** it's tempting to jump straight to the cost to the other person — but naming that first, before you've reckoned with what it costs *you*, tends to produce guilt rather than change. Guilt that arrives before self-understanding usually just makes you defensive or numb. Do the internal part first.

**7a. The Cost to Me — Past / Present / Future**
**What to do:** Write what running this pattern has actually cost *you* — not the other person yet. How has it shown up in your own mind, your own emotional state, how you see yourself? Use three time frames:
- *Past:* a specific moment you saw this cost you something.
- *Present:* what it's costing you right now, ongoing.
- *Future:* if nothing changes, what does this cost you five or ten years from now — be specific, not general.

**7b. The Cost to the Other Person / the Relationship — Past / Present / Future**
**What to do:** Now do the same three time frames, but naming the actual cost to the person this pattern acts on, and to the relationship itself. Be as concrete as you were about yourself.

---

### 8. Anchor Identity
**What it is:** A link to the true identity you're running the new pattern *from* — not from willpower, but from a settled sense of who you are. This comes before the new pattern is written, on purpose.
**What to do:** Name the identity truth (from your Identity Installer work, if you have one, or simply the truth that best answers the fear underneath the old pattern) that the next section needs to be written *from*.

---

### 9. The New Pattern — Step by Step
**What it is:** The specific, rehearsable replacement — matched step for step against the old sequence in section 4, and written *from* the identity named in section 8.
**What to do:** Don't write a value statement ("I want to be more patient"). Write the actual moves: what you notice first, what you do first, second, third instead. It has to be concrete enough to picture yourself doing it, or it can't compete with the old pattern when the real moment comes.

---

### 10. Holding Both — In the Body
**What it is:** A brief practice connecting the old pattern's bodily signal with the new pattern's felt sense, at the same time.
**What to do:** Locate where the old pattern lives in your body — often the chest, jaw, or stomach. Then locate where the new pattern lands when you picture running it instead. One hand on each place. Hold both for 20–30 seconds, breathing normally. Notice what happens at the old place now that the new one is present with it — not after it.

---

### 11. Out Loud Rehearsal — With a Witness
**What it is:** Practicing the New Pattern out loud, with another person present, before you need it in the real moment.
**What to do — basic how-to:**
1. Pick a calm, low-pressure moment — not in the middle of an actual conflict.
2. Say the trigger out loud (section 1).
3. Say the Old Pattern out loud, step by step (section 4), so your witness hears exactly what currently happens.
4. Say the New Pattern out loud, step by step (section 9), as if it's already happening — actually speak the words you'd say.
5. Ask your witness one honest question: *does that sound like the person you're trying to become?*
6. Repeat it two or three times in the same sitting. Fluency comes from repetition, not a single read-through.
7. Afterward, in real life, ask your witness to tell you honestly which pattern actually showed up.

**If this feels too exposed at first:** read the script aloud to yourself alone once before bringing it to your witness — that counts as a first rep, not an avoidance.

**A note if your witness is also affected by this pattern** (a spouse who's watched it happen to your kids, for instance): that's not disqualifying, but it's worth naming directly with them — ask them to distinguish helping you practice from their own feelings about what's happened. If that feels too enmeshed right now, use a different person (a trusted friend, your coach) for the rehearsal itself, and save your original witness's real-life observation for section 13.

**Why this matters:** Reading a plan and being able to run it under pressure are two different skills. Reflection alone rarely produces a fluent alternative response — the research behind this workbook found that a practiced, physically or verbally rehearsed alternative is what actually competes with an automatic pattern, and doing it with another person present is part of what makes the rehearsal work, not an optional extra.

---

### 12. The Repair Step
**What it is:** What you do when the old pattern fires anyway — because it sometimes still will.
**What to do:** Write out, in your own words, exactly what you'll say to the other person afterward. Naming the misstep directly to them — the same day, if possible — is not damage control. It's often the more important lesson: that you're accountable, not infallible, and that ruptures get repaired rather than left standing. *"If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us"* (1 John 1:9, ESV) — this is what a forgiven person can afford to do that a defended one can't.

---

### 13. Witness
**What it is:** Someone positioned to tell you honestly which pattern actually ran — the old one or the new one, and to practice the out-loud rehearsal in section 11 with you.
**What to do:** Name a specific person — usually a spouse or close peer, not the person the pattern acts on. Ask them directly to tell you what they notice, even when it's not flattering.

---

### 14. Practice Rhythm
- **Daily:** Read the New Pattern for whichever entry is most live right now. Picture yourself running it before the triggering situation is likely to occur.
- **Weekly:** Revisit one full entry, including the Cost fields — add anything new you've noticed.
- **As it happens:** When the old pattern fires, go straight to the Repair Step the same day.
- **Over time:** As an entry's old-pattern signal (section 1/4) stops producing a real charge — first in reflection, then in the live moment itself — it's shifting from something you're fighting to something that's simply true now. At that point, the Repair Step (if still needed at all) becomes past-tense testimony rather than a rehearsed script, and Out Loud Rehearsal drops to an occasional spot-check. Note the date when that happens.

## A Completed Example

*(This is illustrative, not a real client's material — yours will be specific to you.)*

**This Activates When:** My spouse brings up an unfinished task in front of others.

**Whose Pattern This Is:** My father's — public correction as the default response to any shortfall.

**Wound or Gap?:** Mostly gap — I was corrected this way constantly and never saw another option modeled, even though I wasn't harshly punished for it.

**The Old Pattern — Step by Step:** 1) Chest tightens, feels like exposure. 2) I respond defensively, often sharply. 3) The conversation becomes about my tone, not the original task. 4) I withdraw for the rest of the evening.

**What This Pattern Gives Me:** It ends the exposed feeling fast. It lets me avoid admitting the task genuinely slipped.

**What Am I Trusting Instead?:** My own image of competence — being seen as someone who doesn't drop things — instead of trusting God with the fact that I'm not always going to be impressive, and that his acceptance of me was never contingent on my performance in the first place.

**Cost to Me:** *Past* — I've spent whole evenings replaying it, ashamed of my own reaction. *Present* — I'm rehearsing defensiveness so often it's becoming my resting posture with people I love. *Future* — in ten years, if this doesn't change, my reflex with anyone I love is combat, not correction.

**Cost to the Relationship:** *Past* — my spouse has started softening or avoiding raising real things with me. *Present* — small issues are going unaddressed because raising them isn't safe. *Future* — a marriage where my spouse manages me instead of speaks freely to me.

**Anchor Identity:** I am secure — my worth isn't determined by never falling short in front of others.

**The New Pattern — Step by Step:** 1) Notice the chest-tightening without acting on it. 2) Take one breath before responding. 3) Say "you're right, that slipped — thank you for naming it" before anything else. 4) Address the task itself once the defensiveness has passed.

**Out Loud Rehearsal:** Practiced the New Pattern with my spouse on a Tuesday evening, unrelated to any actual incident — said the trigger, the old defensive script, then the new one out loud, twice. She said it sounded stiff but real, and asked me to try it again slower.

**Repair Step:** "I got defensive back there instead of just hearing you — I'm sorry. You were right, and I didn't want to just say that."

**Witness:** My spouse, asked directly to name which pattern showed up.
