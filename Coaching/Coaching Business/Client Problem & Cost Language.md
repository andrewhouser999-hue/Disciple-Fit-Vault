# Disciple Fit — Client Problem & Cost Language

**What this is:** The 17 core problems Disciple Fit exists to solve, written in client voice with the felt cost that creates motivation to invest. Built from ICF coaching demand research, counseling-scope research, and Disciple Fit niche analysis.
**Who it's for:** Andy — intake design, marketing copy, discovery call framework, social content, website
**How to use:** Lead with the felt cost in marketing and intake — not the root. The spiritual/identity root is the door you open after the client recognizes their cost.
**Date:** April 2026

---

## The Rule

**People pay when they feel the cost of the problem — not when they see the root.**
Name the felt cost first. Reveal the root second.

---

## Part 1 — Pain Away (Escape Motivation)

*Problems where something is breaking, drifting, or stuck.*

---

### 1. My marriage feels like coexistence, not partnership — and I don't know why

> "We don't fight. We don't connect either. We're roommates who share a bed and a calendar. I thought marriage would feel like something. I'm starting to wonder if this is just what it is — or if something is quietly dying."

**Cost:** Loneliness inside the relationship they built their life around. The fear that the drift is permanent. The slow grief of an unfulfilling marriage they're too committed to leave and too exhausted to fix alone.

---

### 2. I know what I believe but I can't seem to live it

> "I have the theology. I've heard the sermons. I can tell you what's true. But Monday comes and I'm anxious, reactive, and running on empty again. It doesn't translate. And I don't understand why."

**Cost:** Quiet despair that knowledge isn't enough — and the creeping suspicion that something is wrong with them, not just their effort. Leads to disengagement from faith or performance of it.

**Common Experiences** *(Hypothesis-level, patterned from Willard's VIM and Smith's habit/liturgy formation — flag any that don't match what you've actually seen):*

- **The Sermon-Notes Christian** — takes detailed notes, journals the insight, and by Tuesday the truth has evaporated from behavior; the knowing stayed in the notebook.
- **The Apologetics-Fluent Skeptic-of-Self** — can defend the faith to anyone, but privately doubts it has actually changed *them* specifically.
- **The Seminary-Trained Burnout** — formal theological training, fluent in the original languages, and still reacts with the same anger or anxiety as before the training.
- **The Verse-Memorizer Under Pressure** — has the exact verse memorized for the exact situation, and it doesn't come to mind when the moment actually arrives.
- **The Willpower Loop** — treats the gap as a discipline failure, tries harder (more prayer, more accountability, more reading), burns out, and the gap returns unchanged.
- **The Two-Selves Parent or Small-Group Leader** — teaches patience and grace to their kids or group, then reacts the opposite way at home an hour later, and feels like a fraud for the contrast.
- **The Long-Time-Believer Plateau** — decades in the faith, still hits the same reactive patterns as year one; years accumulated haven't produced proportional change in behavior.
- **The Crisis-Only Christian** — the theology holds up fine in ordinary life and collapses precisely when real stress hits, revealing it was assented to but never installed at the level that governs behavior under pressure.

**How These Relate:** All eight are the same mechanism wearing different amounts of apparent spiritual sophistication — the truth sits in the notebook, the argument, the transcript, the memorized verse, the willpower attempt, the teaching moment, or the years accumulated, but has not migrated to what Willard calls the will or heart, the place that actually governs behavior under pressure. Sophistication is not protective here; if anything, the more capable someone is at generating right answers, the more privately shameful the gap feels, because they've run out of the excuse "I just don't know enough yet." Website copy can therefore lead with any of the eight without contradicting the others — a seminary graduate and a casual sermon-notes-taker are describing the identical gap from different vantage points.

### 3. Anxiety and shame that my faith keeps failing to touch

> "I've prayed. I've repented. I've claimed the promises. The anxiety comes back. The shame comes back. I feel like everyone else got something I didn't — some switch that actually works. I'm exhausted from trying."

**Cost:** Spiritual disillusionment. Private hopelessness. The cost of living in a body that won't calm down despite a faith they genuinely hold. Secondary shame: "What's wrong with my faith?"

**Common Experiences** *(Hypothesis-level, patterned from Gendlin's felt sense and Beck/Young's cognitive-schema theory — flag any that don't match what you've actually seen):*

- **The Post-Repentance Relapse** — genuinely repents, feels clean, and within days or hours the same shame wave returns uninvited, as if the repentance never registered anywhere below the conscious decision.
- **The Physically-Anxious Worshiper** — sings truth on Sunday with a racing heart and a tight chest that never actually settles, even as the lyrics affirm peace.
- **The "Good Christians Don't Feel This" Shame Spiral** — has ordinary anxiety, then adds a second layer of shame for having the anxiety at all, since a "strong enough" faith is assumed to prevent it.
- **The Prayer-Fatigued Petitioner** — has prayed the same anxious pattern away so many times that prayer itself now carries dread, because it hasn't worked before.
- **The High-Functioning Anxious Achiever** — nobody around them would guess; performance covers it completely, which deepens the private shame that the "faith is working" image is itself a performance.
- **The Body That Won't Get the Memo** — cognitively believes they're forgiven and secure, and still has a physical startle, freeze, or flush response to reminders of the old failure — the theology and the nervous system are answering different questions.
- **The Comparison Casualty** — watches someone else who "seems to have gotten it" (visible peace, visible confidence) and reads their own unresolved anxiety as proof of a personal defect rather than a common, addressable pattern.
- **The Old-Wound Re-Trigger** — a specific memory, tone of voice, or situation reliably produces the anxiety or shame response regardless of current theological conviction, because the response is anchored to the original moment, not to present belief.

**How These Relate:** These eight cluster around one mechanism: anxiety and shame are stored and triggered somatically and emotionally — a felt sense, in Gendlin's terms, or an automatic schema-level response, in Young's terms — while every tool this person has already tried (repentance, prayer, worship, willpower, comparison-driven effort) operates at the cognitive, behavioral, or devotional layer instead. The mismatch itself, not a deficiency of faith, is why sincere, repeated spiritual effort keeps producing the same result. The felt-cost language that will resonate across all eight is not "I don't believe enough" but "my body hasn't gotten the message my mind already believes."

### 4. I don't know who I am outside of what I do or what others expect

> "I perform well. People have a good image of me. But take away my role, my title, my usefulness — and I genuinely don't know what's left. I don't know what I actually want. I'm not sure I have a self underneath all of it."

**Cost:** Fragile identity that collapses under failure, loss of role, or transition. Relationships feel transactional because they're built on performance. Deep loneliness. Fear of being truly known.

---

### 5. I keep making the same mistakes — nothing actually changes

> "I've repented of this a hundred times. I've made the decision, started strong, and ended up in the same place again. I'm starting to believe I'm just broken in a way that doesn't get fixed."

**Cost:** Eroded self-trust. Hopelessness about real change. Often leads to either spiritual withdrawal ("what's the point") or compulsive religious effort that produces exhaustion without transformation.

---

### 6. I'm isolated — performing fine but known by no one

> "I have people around me. I show up to community. But no one actually knows what's happening inside me. I've never let them. I'm not sure I know how — or if it's safe. So I keep performing okay while feeling completely alone."

**Cost:** The specific pain of loneliness inside community — which feels worse than regular loneliness because it should be different. Leads to hollow relationships, disconnected marriage, and a growing internal life no one has access to.

---

### 7. I feel spiritually stuck and I don't know what's in the way

> "I'm not in crisis. I'm not walking away from God. I'm just... flat. I've been at the same place for years. I do the things. Nothing moves. I don't know if the problem is discipline, or something deeper, or me."

**Cost:** Wasted years. Spiritual boredom masking real blockage. The cost is invisible until a crisis hits and the shallow root system fails — then it's catastrophic. The patient person paying here is the one who senses that and doesn't want to wait.

**Common Experiences** *(Hypothesis-level, patterned from Willard's VIM — the "means" component specifically — flag any that don't match what you've actually seen):*

- **The Faithful-but-Flat Practitioner** — maintains every discipline (devotions, church, service) on schedule, and the sense of aliveness that used to accompany them is simply gone, with no crisis to explain it.
- **The Program-Hopper** — has tried a new Bible plan, a new small group, a new book, a new church program, each producing a short bump followed by the same flatness returning within weeks.
- **The Successful-Life, Flat-Faith Split** — everything else in life (career, marriage, health) is going reasonably well, which makes the spiritual flatness more confusing rather than less, since there's no obvious cause to point to.
- **The Long-Tenured Believer Coasting** — years of maturity and leadership experience, running competently on stored knowledge and habit, aware the engine isn't actually running even though the outputs still look fine to everyone else.
- **The Post-Breakthrough Plateau** — had a real, dateable spiritual high point (a retreat, a renewal, a specific answered prayer) years ago, and has been quietly plateaued at that same level since, treating the plateau as the new normal rather than a stall.
- **The Self-Diagnosing Doubter** — cycles through blaming discipline ("I'm not doing enough"), then sin ("something unconfessed is blocking this"), then doubt ("maybe I never really had it"), without landing on an actual answer, because none of the available self-diagnoses is the real mechanism.
- **The Quietly-Waiting Christian** — isn't in crisis, isn't walking away, isn't asking for help yet — just privately aware, maybe for years, that "this is probably not what it's supposed to feel like," without knowing what to do with that awareness.
- **The Isolated-Growth Plateau** — has been doing all the "right" things privately or in shallow community, and the plateau is arguably the predictable result of growth attempted outside the relational context growth actually requires.

**How These Relate:** All eight describe the same missing piece in Willard's VIM framework in different disguises — the vision (wanting more) and the intention (still trying) are intact in every case; what's missing or misapplied is the actual means of change, whether that's relational infrastructure, felt-level rather than purely informational engagement, or an accurate diagnosis of what's actually blocking movement. The self-diagnosing doubter's failed loop (discipline → sin → doubt) is instructive: it shows the person has already ruled out the wrong causes through their own trial and error, which makes them exactly the audience most ready to recognize a different, more accurate diagnosis when it's offered with precision instead of another discipline plan.

### 8. I'm burned out on ministry or leadership and losing faith in the process

> "I built my life around serving. I believed in it. Now I resent it. I'm going through the motions, protecting my image, and privately questioning everything. I'm afraid to say that out loud to anyone inside the church."

**Cost:** Vocational collapse. Spiritual crisis. Relational isolation — they can't be honest with the people most likely to help because those people are their congregation or team. The loneliness of leading while falling apart.

---

### 9. I'm heading into a major life transition with no solid foundation

> "Everything is about to change — marriage, a new role, a move, a loss. I know I should feel ready. I don't. I don't trust myself under pressure. I don't have a clear enough sense of who I am to know how I'll hold up."

**Cost:** Entering a defining season unprepared. The fear that the cracks they've managed in normal life will become fractures under pressure. Marriage prep, leadership transition, and parenting launch are all high here.

---

### 10. The gap between my Sunday self and Monday self is widening

> "Who I am at church and who I am the rest of the week are becoming two different people. I know it. It's starting to feel dishonest. But I don't know how to close the gap — and I'm not sure anyone around me is actually living differently."

**Cost:** Integrity erosion. Private cynicism about faith. The gap, left unaddressed, produces either full disengagement from church or full performance of it — neither of which is life. This is the slow drift toward nominal Christianity.

**Common Experiences** *(Hypothesis-level, patterned from Kegan & Lahey's Immunity to Change and Smith's cultural liturgies — flag any that don't match what you've actually seen):*

- **The Compartmentalized Professional** — genuinely worships Sunday, then operates by a harsher, more anxious, more self-protective set of rules at work Monday through Friday, without ever consciously deciding to.
- **The Private-Public Split Parent** — teaches their kids biblical character at church or in home devotions, and privately notices they don't actually live those values under normal weekday stress like traffic, deadlines, or conflict.
- **The Values-Speech, Behavior-Mismatch Leader** — can articulate their values fluently to a team or small group, and privately knows their actual weekday decisions don't consistently track those stated values.
- **The Silent Comparer** — sees other church members posting or performing the put-together Christian life, assumes they're the only one with the gap, and hides it further rather than naming it.
- **The Two-Calendar Christian** — Sunday's calendar (church, rest, family) and Monday's calendar (hustle, image management, self-reliance) run on two different assumptions about what actually secures them, and the person has never noticed the assumptions conflict.
- **The Growing-Cynicism Case** — started noticing the gap years ago, hasn't closed it, and has quietly downgraded expectations for what church or faith actually does, becoming a nominal or checked-out version of what used to be full engagement.
- **The Autopilot Marriage-or-Family Christian** — the widening isn't dramatic or crisis-driven; it's a slow drift where the Monday self has been running unexamined for years on autopilot while the Sunday self stays the officially maintained identity.
- **The Threshold-Moment Noticer** — a specific week (a hard conversation, a moment of real anger, a compromise made under pressure) suddenly makes the gap undeniable, even though it's been present the whole time — the moment doesn't create the gap, it just makes it visible.

**How These Relate:** Kegan and Lahey's framework explains why none of these people can simply try harder to close the gap — the Monday behavior isn't a discipline failure but the visible output of a hidden competing commitment (self-protection, control, image management, security through performance) running underneath the stated Sunday commitment, itself resting on an unexamined assumption about what will actually keep them safe. What varies across the eight is only how visible or costly the gap has become, from barely-noticed drift to an undeniable threshold moment — useful for messaging, because it means content can meet someone at any point on that visibility spectrum and still be describing the same underlying mechanism.

## Part 2 — Gain Toward (Aspiration Motivation)

*Problems where something is beginning and the person wants to enter it well.*

---

### 11. I'm stepping into a leadership role and I don't trust myself at this level

> "I got the promotion. The title. The responsibility. And the first thing I felt wasn't excitement — it was dread. I don't know if I actually have what it takes. I've been faking competence at the lower level for a while. Now the stakes are real and I can't afford to fake it."

**Cost:** The gap between the role and the identity that inhabits it. Leaders who don't close this gap either perform and burn out, or shrink and underdeliver. For Christians: the added weight of "God gave me this" makes the stakes feel even higher.

---

### 12. I'm getting married and I want to enter it whole, not just hopeful

> "I love this person. I want this. But I also know I carry things — patterns, wounds, ways of shutting down or overreacting — that I haven't dealt with. I don't want to bring that into the marriage and spend the first five years figuring out what I should have figured out before."

**Cost:** Entering the most important covenant relationship carrying unexamined baggage. The cost is paid later — in conflict cycles, emotional distance, and patterns that compound over years. Clients here are paying to prevent a future cost rather than escape a present one.

---

### 13. I want to be a different kind of parent than I had — and I don't know how

> "I swore I wouldn't do what was done to me. And then I hear my father's words come out of my mouth. Or I check out the same way my mother did. I don't want to pass this down. But wanting it to stop and knowing how to stop it are two completely different things."

**Cost:** Generational transmission. The thing they survived becoming the thing their child grows up in. For Christians: the theological weight of generational patterns makes this even more urgent and personal.

---

### 14. I sense a calling I haven't had the courage or clarity to pursue

> "There's something I keep coming back to. A direction I feel pulled toward. I've dismissed it, prayed it away, told myself it's not practical. But it won't leave. And I'm starting to realize the problem isn't the calling — it's that I don't believe I'm the kind of person it happens to."

**Cost:** The unlived life. Not dramatic — quiet. The slow accumulation of years spent building something safe while the real thing waits. For Christians: the theological weight of stewardship — "what did you do with what I gave you?"

---

### 15. I want my life to actually mean something — and I'm not building toward that

> "I'm not depressed. I'm not in crisis. I'm just... going through motions that don't add up to anything I care about. I do the next thing. Then the next thing. And occasionally I stop and think — is this it? Am I building toward something? Because it doesn't feel like it."

**Cost:** Accumulated meaninglessness. Years of competent, successful motion that doesn't compound into anything that matters. For Christians this is especially sharp: they believe life has purpose — they just can't find the thread of it in their actual week.

---

### 16. I'm launching something and the internal resistance is the problem

> "I've planned it. I know the steps. I've started three times. Every time I get close to real exposure — putting it out, asking for the sale, stepping into the room — something shuts down. I tell myself it's the strategy. But I know it's me. I'm the obstacle."

**Cost:** The idea that never becomes the thing. The ministry that stays a vision. The business that exists in a Google doc. The book that's been "almost ready" for two years. The growing suspicion that they'll always find a reason to stop short.

---

### 17. I know what I need to do — I just can't do it alone

> "I don't need someone to tell me what to do. I've read the books. I know the framework. What I need is someone who will actually hold me to it. Someone who asks the hard question, sees through the excuse, and doesn't let me off the hook. I've never had that."

**Cost:** Wasted self-knowledge. This person is unusually self-aware — which makes it more painful, not less. They can see exactly what they're not doing and why. For Christians: this is also a discipleship deficit — the absence of anyone who knows them well enough to speak into their actual life.

---

## What the Full List Reveals

Across all 17 problems, two things are almost always true:

1. **The real problem is identity** — who they believe they are, what they believe they deserve, what they believe is possible for them specifically
2. **The real deficit is relationship** — no one safe enough, honest enough, equipped enough to go there with them

That is Disciple Fit's entire value proposition in two sentences.

## Cross-Problem Relationship — #2, #3, #7, #10 (Website Entry-Point Design)

*Added 2026-09-09 at Andrew's request, to serve as the working basis for website messaging — see the Common Experiences and How These Relate sections added to #2, #3, #7, and #10 above.*

All four are variations on a single deeper pattern: sincerely held Christian belief that has not migrated into the layer that actually governs feeling (#3), behavior (#2, #10), or a felt sense of aliveness (#7). None of the four is a knowledge problem — the person already has, or has had, real conviction — and none is solved by more information, willpower, or effort in the same register that has already been tried and has already failed.

- **#2** names the mechanism generally: knowing versus living.
- **#3** names it in the emotional and somatic domain specifically: belief doesn't reach the body.
- **#7** names it as an absence of felt aliveness with no identifiable cause.
- **#10** names it as a visible behavioral split between two contexts, Sunday and Monday.

A person could recognize themselves in one, several, or all four — they are four different windows onto the same underlying gap between what is believed and what is actually operative, which is precisely the identity-level root Disciple Fit and CIC treat, and precisely why the four function well together as parallel entry points into the same site.

**Website UX implication:** Structure the site so a visitor can enter through whichever of the roughly 32 common-experience statements above (or the four umbrella problems) lands with the most precision — for example, a set of short, specific recognition statements such as "You know what you believe and still can't seem to live it," "The anxiety comes back no matter how much you've prayed," "You're doing everything right and still feel flat," or "Who you are at church and who you are Monday through Friday are starting to feel like two different people" — each functioning as its own doorway. Regardless of entry point, the path converges on the same next step (the discovery call, the CIC offer), because all four ultimately point to the same identity-level root. This matches the Marketing Charter's Stage 2 "Recognition" mechanic — precision of language naming the exact interior experience — multiplied across enough specific variations that more visitors find their own words already written for them, without diluting the site into targeting everyone generically. Per the Marketing Charter's Governing Rule, none of these entry statements should name the root (identity, the institutional wound, sin patterns) — each stays at the felt-cost layer; the root is revealed only after the visitor has chosen an entry point and moved deeper into the site.

**Before building this into actual site copy or navigation:** the Common Experiences above are Hypothesis-level, generated from established formation/psychology frameworks, not yet tested against real client or prospect language. Recommend running these past actual discovery-call notes, past client intake language, or a small round of prospect feedback before locking any of them into permanent site copy — per the CIC Target Market Definition & Messaging Generator's rule against presenting confident, well-written psychographic detail that isn't yet confirmed true of anyone.
