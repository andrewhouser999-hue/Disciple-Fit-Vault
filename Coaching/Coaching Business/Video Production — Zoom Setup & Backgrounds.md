# Video Production — Zoom Setup & Backgrounds

Practical guidance for what to put behind and in front of the camera during client-facing Zoom sessions (coaching or advisory), for use when the room available doesn't already look appealing on camera.

## Core rule: skip Zoom's virtual background feature

Zoom's AI-composited virtual backgrounds (a fake image placed behind you) produce visible edge artifacts around hair and shoulders and flicker when you move. Clients register this — even when the artifacts are subtle — as slightly artificial, which works against trust in exactly the moments where trust is the product: coaching disclosure, advisory candor. The read a client gets, even subconsciously, is "this person didn't bother to actually be here." Avoid the virtual-image feature entirely for client sessions.

## Preferred option: real background, blurred

Zoom's *blur background* setting is different from a virtual image — it keeps the actual room and its real depth and light in the shot and only softens it, similar to a shallow depth-of-field on a real camera. It does not composite a fake image, so it doesn't produce the edge-artifact or flicker problem. This is the default fix when the room behind you is boring or cluttered but a fake backdrop isn't worth the trust cost.

## Better option: stage a narrow slice of real background

A full appealing room isn't necessary — only about a 4-foot-wide slice directly behind you needs to look intentional, since that's all the camera actually captures. In rough order of effort:

- **Reposition to face a plain wall.** Add one framed piece of art, or a small shelf with three or four objects (books, a plant — nothing branded or cluttered). Keep the arrangement asymmetric rather than centered on the frame, which reads as more natural and less like a staged backdrop.
- **Add a fabric backdrop on a tension rod.** Muslin or a matte neutral fabric (charcoal, sage, or warm gray) clipped to a tension rod behind the chair costs roughly $30–50, rolls up when not in session, and gives a consistent, professional-looking backdrop without needing to renovate the room. Avoid pure black (crushes detail on most webcams) and pure white (blows out and washes out skin tone).
- **Bring a small bookshelf or credenza into frame.** Even two shelves staged specifically for the camera read as "office" to a client who only sees the slice on screen, regardless of what the rest of the room looks like.

## Lighting matters more than the background itself

A dim or backlit room makes any background — real, blurred, or staged — look worse, and makes the presenter look worse, which affects client trust more than what's behind them. A $25–40 ring light or small softbox placed in front of the presenter, at eye level or slightly above, resolves most "this setup looks unprofessional" impressions before the background is even addressed. If lighting budget is limited, prioritize a light purchase over a backdrop purchase — the return on trust and perceived professionalism is higher.

## Tone by practice track

Per [[feedback_practice_separation|Three-Track Separation]], background and lighting choices should match the register of the practice the session belongs to:

- **RPA (owner/sponsor-facing, corporate advisory):** blur, or a plain wall with one neutral object. Nothing personal or devotional should be visible in frame — the register is corporate advisory, not pastoral.
- **CIC / coaching (pastoral, relational):** a slightly warmer setup is appropriate — a plant, a warm-toned wall, or a soft lamp instead of a clinical light — since some visible humanity in the space supports rather than undercuts the relational work.
