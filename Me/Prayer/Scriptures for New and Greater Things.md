# Scriptures for New and Greater Things

Seven passages for praying into new ventures, expanded scope, and God-sized undertakings — grouped by movement: letting go of the old, asking for enlargement, depending on the Spirit rather than personal capacity, and surrendering the outcome. ESV throughout.

---

## 1. Not despising the beginning — Zechariah 4:6, 10

**Text & Context.** Zerubbabel is leading the second-temple rebuild post-exile. Progress has stalled; the people have grown discouraged comparing this small foundation to Solomon's temple. God's word to him through the vision of the lampstand: *"Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit, says the LORD of hosts... For who has despised the day of small things?"*

**Language.** "Might" (*ḥayil*) carries the sense of collective strength — an army, a workforce. "Power" (*kōaḥ*) is the strength of a single potent individual. Together they cover both modes of human effort: mass mobilization and personal capability. God is explicitly ruling both out as the mechanism.

**Theological weight.** This isn't anti-effort — Zerubbabel still has to lay stones. It's a redefinition of what's actually load-bearing in the work. The temple gets finished, but the text insists the causal credit goes to the Spirit, not the org chart.

**Prayer application.** Use this when the new thing feels small, underfunded, or embarrassingly early-stage compared to what you're picturing it becoming. The prayer isn't "give me more might and power" — it's asking to *not despise* the current smallness, and to locate your confidence in the Spirit's sufficiency rather than your own resourcing.

## 2. Letting go of the former thing — Isaiah 43:18-19

**Text & Context.** Second Isaiah, addressed to exiles in Babylon who are discouraged and doubting God still acts. God recalls the Exodus (the "former things") only to say He isn't limited to repeating it: *"Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?"*

**Language.** The Hebrew for "new" (*ḥadash*) signals something genuinely unprecedented, not a rerun of a prior pattern. The command "remember not" is better read as *don't let the former things anchor your expectation of what I can do* — not literal amnesia, since Isaiah elsewhere commands active remembrance of the Exodus. It's a redirection of attention, not an erasure.

**Interpretive note.** Some commentators read a messianic/incarnational trajectory into "new thing" as ultimate fulfillment; that's a legitimate canonical extension but goes beyond what the immediate historical referent (return from exile) asserts. Worth knowing the layer you're praying from.

**Prayer application.** This is the passage for when your own prior pattern of what's "worked before" is quietly capping your expectation of the new venture. The prayer is for eyes to perceive what's already springing up rather than default-measuring the new thing against the old one.

## 3. A work you wouldn't believe if told — Habakkuk 1:5

**Text & Context.** God's answer to Habakkuk's complaint about injustice: *"Look among the nations, and see; wonder and be astounded. For I am doing a work in your days that you would not believe if told."* Worth noting soberly: the "work" here is the rise of Babylon as judgment — a hard providence, not simple encouragement. Using this verse devotionally for a hoped-for expansion is a legitimate application-by-analogy, but it's not the verse's original referent, and that gap is worth being honest about if it's ever taught to someone else.

**Prayer application.** Best used narrowly — as language for "God, do something I currently cannot picture," not as a general-purpose promise of favorable outcome. Pair it with Ephesians 3 below, which actually promises good.

---

## 4. Beyond what you can ask or think — Ephesians 3:20-21

**Text & Context.** The doxology closing Paul's prayer for the Ephesians' inner strengthening. *"Now to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think, according to the power at work within us..."*

**Language.** The Greek is a stacked superlative — *hyperekperissou* (itself a compound of "beyond" + "exceedingly" + "abundantly") layered onto *hyperballō* ("to throw beyond," i.e., surpass). Wuest calls it "a superlative of superlatives." The phrase occurs almost nowhere else in the NT — Paul is straining the language to say the best-case scenario is not the ceiling.

**Theological weight — the qualifier that matters.** The clause "according to the power at work within us" is not decorative. It ties the abundance to the Spirit's indwelling power (named explicitly two verses earlier, 3:16), not to ambition, faith-as-technique, or the size of the ask. MacArthur's reading is worth taking seriously: this promise describes what God *can* do through a Spirit-filled, Christ-indwelt person — it's not an unconditional blank check independent of that condition.

**Prayer application.** This is the passage for the moment of catching yourself scaling down the prayer because the ask feels presumptuous. It reframes: the limit isn't God's capacity, it's whether you're asking small out of false humility. Pray it *with* the Zechariah 4 caveat, not instead of it — abundance flows through the Spirit's power, not around the need for dependence.

## 5. The caution passage — 1 Chronicles 4:10 (Jabez)

**Text & Context.** A one-verse genealogical aside: *"Jabez called upon the God of Israel, saying, 'Oh that you would bless me and enlarge my border, and that your hand might be with me, and that you would keep me from harm so that it might not bring me pain!' And God granted what he asked."*

**Interpretive debate — flagged deliberately.** This verse became the center of a bestselling prosperity-adjacent devotional in the early 2000s, and that popularization is worth knowing because it shapes how the verse *sounds* today even when that reading isn't intended. Sound commentary reads "enlarge my border" against the conquest context — Jabez is asking for what God already promised the tribes and commanded them to possess, not for personal accumulation. The prayer's shape (bless me / enlarge / your hand with me / keep me from harm) subordinates the enlargement request to God's presence and protection, not the reverse.

**Prayer application.** Legitimate to pray for expanded scope using this verse's own vocabulary — but keep the order Jabez used: *your hand with me* is the load-bearing request; enlarged territory is what that presence produces, not a separate ask made of God to serve. If this ever migrates into a client-facing or RPA document, the prosperity-gospel misreading needs to be named and fenced off explicitly.

## 6. Enlarging the tent — Isaiah 54:2-3

**Text & Context.** Addressed to Zion personified as a barren woman who will yet have more children than the married woman (54:1) — a picture of post-exilic restoration and, canonically, the church's expansion (Paul cites 54:1 in Galatians 4:27). *"Enlarge the place of your tent... lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes. For you will spread abroad to the right and to the left."*

**Language.** The imagery is nomadic tent enlargement — driving stakes further out *before* the population that will fill the space has arrived. The command to enlarge precedes the fulfillment; faith-postured preparation, not reactive scaling.

**Prayer application.** Useful specifically for the practical, structural side of a new venture — office space, capacity, systems, hiring — prayed as an act of faith that outruns current evidence, paired with "strengthen your stakes" so the prayer isn't just for expansion but for the stability to hold it.

---

## 7. Establishing the work of your hands — Psalm 90:17

**Text & Context.** Moses' psalm, the only one attributed to him, closing a meditation on human transience (90:1-12) with a plea for God's favor on present labor: *"Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us, and establish the work of our hands upon us; yes, establish the work of our hands!"*

**Language.** The repetition of "establish" (*kûn* — to fix firmly, make permanent) in the same breath is emphatic doubling, a Hebrew intensifier. Coming right after a psalm about how fleeting human life is (90:5-6, "like grass... in the evening it fades"), the prayer is essentially: my days are short and this effort could evaporate with me — make it outlast that.

**Prayer application.** The closing prayer, not the opening one. Once the former pattern is released (Isaiah 43), confidence is located in the Spirit not might (Zechariah 4), the ask is made in step with His presence (Jabez), and the tent is enlarged (Isaiah 54) — this is what to pray over the actual work itself: that it holds, that it isn't wasted effort against the grain of mortality.

---

## Woven together as one prayer

*Lord, I release my confidence in what worked before — I don't want the former things to cap what I expect You can do. I don't despise this day of small things; my hope isn't in my own might or power but in Your Spirit. I ask You to enlarge the space I'm preparing, and to strengthen what holds it up. Let Your hand be with me in this, that the enlargement wouldn't bring harm but genuine blessing. Do far more than I am asking or thinking, according to the power already at work in me — not around my dependence on You, but through it. And whatever comes of this work, establish it. Let it hold.*

---

**Note on scope.** John 14:12 ("greater works than these") was deliberately left out — its "greater" is quantitative (more people reached post-Pentecost, not qualitatively superior miracles) and is easy to misapply to personal ambition in a way the text doesn't support.
