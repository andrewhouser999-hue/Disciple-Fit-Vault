# Formation & Transition — Declared vs. Felt Qualities of God — Congregation Exercise

## Purpose

A single teaching moment (10–12 minutes) that lets a room *notice in themselves*, rather than be told about, the gap between what they can say about God and what they actually feel when they sit with it. The exercise doesn't explain the gap first and then illustrate it — it produces the gap experientially, so the teaching that follows lands on something people just felt happen to them, not a concept they're asked to take on faith.

## When to Use

- A sermon-adjacent teaching moment, small-group opener, or retreat session on the difference between believed and lived faith.
- Single-session device. It is not the depth of a multi-week small-group curriculum, and it is not a substitute for pastoral care or counseling if it surfaces something a person needs to talk through afterward.
- Not the same tool as Beholding Together — this is a brief noticing exercise for a general room, not a trauma-processing practice for a screened, leader-trained small group. Do not treat what surfaces here as something to work through live in a large-group setting.

## Theological Anchor

Scripture names this exact gap directly, which is why it can be introduced as a biblical category rather than a psychological one:

> "This people draw near with their mouth and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me." (Isaiah 29:13, ESV)

> "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me." (Matthew 15:8, ESV — Jesus quoting Isaiah 29:13 directly)

The exercise's closing point rests on Ezekiel's promise that this is a condition God addresses, not a fixed verdict on anyone in the room:

> "And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you." (Ezekiel 36:26, ESV)

## What's Actually Happening (for the facilitator, not spoken aloud)

What a person can *say* about God and what they actually *feel* about God come from different places in them. One is what they've been taught and can put into words on request. The other was shaped over a whole lifetime — including relationships that had nothing to do with God — and it doesn't automatically update just because a person's stated beliefs have. That's why someone can sincerely say "God is loving" and still feel something closer to distance, suspicion, or nothing at all when they actually sit with the word applied to themselves. This isn't hypocrisy and it isn't a faith failure; it's two different systems in a person that don't always move together. (Research basis, not for the room: Rizzuto's "living God vs. theologian's God" distinction and Hall's implicit/explicit God-image research — see Sources below. This vocabulary stays in the facilitator's own preparation; the script below uses no clinical or academic language.)

## Materials Needed

- One index card or small sheet of paper and a pen per person.
- A quiet room, or at least a moment where the room can go quiet — this exercise does not work well over background noise or in a rushed transition.
- Optional: a visible clock or quiet timer, since the pacing matters (each step is short on purpose — long enough to feel something, not long enough to start analyzing it).

## Facilitator Script

Read the numbered steps as a sequence; the quoted lines are suggested spoken language, not a rigid script — adapt the wording to your own voice while keeping the instruction underneath each one intact.

**1. Frame (30 seconds)**
"We're going to do something a little different for a few minutes. There's often a difference between what we *know* about God and what we actually *feel* when we sit with it — this is a chance to notice that in yourself. Nobody will be asked to share anything out loud. This is between you and God."

**2. Name (2 minutes)**
"Take your card. Write down the first three words or short phrases that come to mind when you think of God — not what you think you're supposed to write, just whatever surfaces first. A quality, a title, an image — whatever comes."
Give the full two minutes even if people finish early; don't rush this step, since the "first thing that comes to mind" instruction is what keeps this from turning into a theology quiz.

**3. Settle (1 minute)**
"Now set the card down for a moment. Let's just get quiet together. Close your eyes if that helps you focus. Take a few slow breaths." (Lead 3–4 slow breaths audibly, then let the room sit in silence for the remainder of the minute.)

**4. Feel each one (about 90 seconds per word — repeat for all three)**
"Pick up your card and look at your first word. Silently say to yourself, 'God is ___ toward me' — put your own name or 'me' in there, not people in general. Now just notice what happens in your body when you sit with that — your chest, your stomach, your shoulders. Is there warmth? Tightness? Nothing at all? Some distance? Don't judge it or explain it to yourself — just notice it, and next to that word, write one or two words for what you noticed, not an explanation of it."
Repeat this same instruction for word two and word three, pausing the full 90 seconds each time. Resist filling the silence — the noticing happens in the quiet, not while you're talking.

**5. Compare (1–2 minutes)**
"Now look at your card. You have three words you named, and next to each one, what you actually felt. Just look at them side by side for a moment. Where do they match? Where's there a gap?"

**6. Close (2–3 minutes, spoken)**
"If you found a gap somewhere on your card, that's common — it's not a sign your faith is failing. What we can say about God and what we've come to feel about God are shaped differently: one by what we've been taught, the other by a whole lifetime of experience, some of which had nothing to do with God at all. Scripture itself names this — the prophet Isaiah spoke of people who 'honor God with their lips' while their 'heart is far' from him, and Jesus quoted that same line centuries later because the pattern hadn't gone away. But Scripture doesn't leave it there — God's own promise is to give a new heart, not just correct information (Ezekiel 36:26). That's not something this exercise resolves in the next three minutes; it's something formed over time, in relationship with him. Take your card with you this week. If you want, bring whatever you noticed to God directly, in your own words — that's a better next step than trying to talk yourself into feeling differently."
Close in prayer or transition directly into the rest of the teaching, as fits your setting. No card should be collected or read by anyone but the person who wrote it, unless someone volunteers to share.

## Facilitator Notes & Cautions

- **Non-clinical, single-session scope.** This is a noticing exercise, not a diagnostic tool and not trauma processing. If someone becomes visibly distressed during or after the exercise, use your church's standard pastoral care-team handoff rather than processing it in front of the group.
- **No disclosure is ever required.** Nobody reads their card aloud unless they choose to. The exercise works because it's private — announcing this explicitly at the start (Step 1) is load-bearing, not optional.
- **Pace matters more than content here.** The instinct will be to rush Steps 3–4 to keep the room comfortable with silence; resist that. The felt noticing this exercise is built on doesn't happen in the first ten seconds of quiet.
- **Frame it as common before you run it, not just after.** If your teaching that day already establishes that formation is slow and gradual rather than resolved in one sitting, people are far less likely to leave Step 6 feeling like the gap on their card is a personal failure.

## Sources

- Rizzuto, A.M. (1979). *The Birth of the Living God.* University of Chicago Press. — the "living God" (functionally experienced) vs. "theologian's God" (propositionally held) distinction underlying Steps 2 and 4.
- Hall, T.W. & Fujikawa, A. (2009). "Attachment to God and Implicit Spirituality." *Journal of Psychology and Theology.* — empirical implicit/explicit God-image research; the basis for treating "what I'd say" and "what I feel" as two genuinely separate systems rather than one system with a reporting error.
- Gendlin, E.T. (1981). *Focusing* (2nd ed.). Bantam Books. — the underlying attend-and-notice method behind Step 4's instruction to notice bodily sensation without immediately explaining or judging it, simplified here for a one-time group setting rather than the full multi-step individual practice.
