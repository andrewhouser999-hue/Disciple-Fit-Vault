# Relational Support Failures in Friend, Family & Christian Community

Ordinary, everyday ways well-meaning people — including well-meaning Christians — fail to actually support someone who is struggling or suffering. Not a research document; a working list for personal reflection and recognition.

## Fixing instead of listening

- Jumping straight to advice or solutions before the person's even finished talking
- "Have you tried…" as the first response to someone venting
- Trying to talk someone out of their feelings ("you shouldn't feel that way")
- Turning every conversation about pain into a problem-solving session, even when they just wanted to be heard

## Minimizing

- "At least…" statements that skip past the loss to find a silver lining
- "It could be worse"
- "Everything happens for a reason"
- Comparing their pain to someone else's ("well, at least it's not cancer")
- Treating a big deal to them as a small deal because it wouldn't be a big deal to you

## Christian-specific versions of the above

- "God's got a plan" delivered as a conversation-ender, not a comfort
- "God won't give you more than you can handle" (which isn't even what that verse says)
- Quoting Romans 8:28 five minutes into someone's grief
- Assuming or hinting that their suffering means they did something wrong or lack faith (Job's friends, basically)
- "I'll pray for you" as the entire response, with no follow-up, no meal, no phone call, no actual showing up
- Telling someone how they should feel about their suffering spiritually ("you should be grateful," "trust God more") instead of just sitting with them in it
- Treating anger at God, doubt, or lament as spiritually immature or off-limits, when Scripture itself is full of it (half the Psalms)

## Making it about themselves

- "I know exactly how you feel — when I went through my divorce…" and then talking for ten minutes about their own experience
- Needing to be comforted themselves, so the suffering person ends up managing their friend's emotions on top of their own
- Asking a lot of questions because they're curious, not because it helps the other person
- Telling other people about it ("prayer request") without asking first — this one is huge and happens constantly in churches

## Disappearing / inconsistent presence

- All-in during the funeral week, meal train and texts, then gone a month later when the grief is actually hardest
- Only checking in right after the crisis, not months later when everyone else has moved on but the person hasn't
- Getting uncomfortable and quietly pulling away because they don't know what to say
- "Thinking of you!" texts that never turn into an actual visit or call
- Support that dries up if the person doesn't seem to be "getting better" fast enough

## Rushing the timeline

- "Isn't it time to move on?"
- Getting impatient or subtly annoyed if someone brings up the same grief again six months later
- Acting surprised or frustrated when someone who seemed "fine" has a bad day again
- Wanting them to hurry up and get back to normal so things aren't awkward anymore

## Language that shuts people down

- "That's not very Christian of you" (said about doubt, anger, or grief)
- "Just have more faith"
- Using something they confided in you later as ammo in an argument or a "gentle" correction
- Responding to vulnerability with a lecture

## Silence-discomfort

- Filling every quiet moment with chatter because *they* can't sit in the silence, not because the sufferer needs noise
- Changing the subject to something lighter because the heavy conversation is uncomfortable for them

## The common thread

Almost all of these come down to the same thing: the "helper" is managing *their own* discomfort — with silence, with not having an answer, with someone else's pain going on too long — rather than actually staying present to what the other person needs.
